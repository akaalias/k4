# Five corpora find no K4 source text

No published text was found that contains "east northeast" and "Berlin clock" close together, and none pairs "east northeast" with the Weltzeituhr or the Mengenlehreuhr in a single passage. Across the Internet Archive full-text index, 44 Project Gutenberg texts, 39 HathiTrust full-text queries over all items (search-only in-copyright volumes included), one Google Books query and many web searches, every document with both cribs is a post-2020 Kryptos write-up quoting Sanborn's clues. The rest are coincidences: dictionaries listing the two words as separate headwords, or a 1919 Army photo catalogue with the phrases 110 pages apart. The phrase "Berlin clock" barely existed in English print before 1990. No indexed volume uses it for the Alexanderplatz World Clock, so BERLINCLOCK looks like Sanborn's own coinage, not a borrowed phrase. That fits everything Sanborn has said: he wrote the text himself ("fiction writings of mine", "the last thing I wrote was on that drive"). Kobek and Byrne, the only outsiders known to have read the recovered plaintext, called it English that "makes semantic sense". They have never called it a quotation. For the autoresearch project this closes the K3-style shortcut in practice. No outside text will hand over extra plaintext, so the method search has to stand on cryptanalysis plus the 24 known letters. Two gaps remain open but are cheap to close. Google Books was blocked after one query. Most 1989 press archives and the in-window spy novels need a login or a purchase.

## Every corpus with both cribs points back to Kryptos itself

The model for this search is K3, which is a tightened paraphrase of Howard Carter's 1923 account of opening Tutankhamun's tomb. If K4 worked the same way, a 1986–1990 text should exist with "east northeast" about 29 letters before "Berlin clock". The October 2026 pass searched the Internet Archive's full-text index, the largest corpus reachable here and rich in 1980s guidebooks, magazines and spy fiction. **Only one document there holds both exact phrases: a 2026 Kryptos analysis upload** whose line "TRUE COMPASS BEARING EAST NORTHEAST — FOLLOW THE SHADOW TO THE FACE OF THE BERLIN CLOCK" is a made-up candidate plaintext, not a source ([Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/kryptos-full-intelligence-cryptographic-analysis)). The Gutenberg grep covered about 22 million letters: Carter, Carnarvon, Amelia Edwards, Budge, Duff Gordon, *In and Around Berlin*, and classic spy and adventure fiction. **It found EASTNORTHEAST once (Gulliver's "Our course was east-north-east") and BERLINCLOCK zero times.** Carter's volume 1 has no "east-northeast" at all, which argues against K4 continuing the Carter source. The web gave the same result. The only pages pairing the phrases are Kryptos pages offering 97-letter candidates, such as the gist debunking a ChatGPT "solution" ([GitHub gist](https://gist.github.com/atoponce/1cd01f40786ab21af011f0d6257fba0c)).

This pass added HathiTrust and Google Books. **HathiTrust worked through a browser and took 39 queries.** It ignores hyphens and case in phrases, and its AND is a true AND. It has no proximity operator, so a volume count is an upper bound, and a zero means the pair appears nowhere in the volume.

The single volume with both phrases is the *Catalogue of official A.E.F. photographs* (1919). "Race to Berlin clock" there is a WWI prison-camp sign on p. 363 ([in-volume search](https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt/search?q1=%22berlin+clock%22&id=njp.32101073332502)), and "looking east northeast; (azimuth 70)" is a French panorama on p. 473 ([in-volume search](https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt/search?q1=%22east+northeast%22&id=njp.32101073332502)). The only non-reference 1980–1990 volume pairing a compass bearing with "Weltzeituhr" is six months of a German jewellers' trade journal from 1986. Its page positions could not be read, and a German watch-trade magazine is a poor source for an English paraphrase. It is not a candidate.

**Google Books got through exactly one query before rate-limiting shut it down.** The anonymous Books API returns a quota of zero, which will not reset by waiting. The HTML search served one page and then showed a CAPTCHA for at least ten minutes. That page, `"east northeast" "berlin clock"`, held two Kryptos books (Dunin & Schmeh 2023, Dooley 2024) and loose matches where Google had dropped the quotes: Krakatoa sky reports of 1888, an 1873 guide to northeast France, and a 1956 bomber memoir that "headed north-east for Berlin" soon after 8 o'clock ([Google Books search](https://www.google.com/search?tbm=bks&q=%22east+northeast%22+%22berlin+clock%22)). None is a candidate, but page 2, the date-limited run and eight other pairings are **not tested**.

## "Berlin clock" was not a name for the World Clock before Sanborn

The stronger finding is about vocabulary, not absence. Google's Ngram corpus shows **"Berlin clock" in English print in only six years before 1991** (1962, 1966, 1974, 1978, 1987, 1988), at a peak of about 5 in 10 billion words ([Ngram "Berlin clock"](https://books.google.com/ngrams/json?content=Berlin+clock&year_start=1950&year_end=2019&corpus=en&smoothing=0)). HathiTrust shows what those rare uses meant. Its 27 "Berlin clock" volumes from 1960–1995 are mostly Sotheby's and Connoisseur listings of Berlin-made antique clocks, and **not one of its 158 "Berlin clock" volumes mentions the Weltzeituhr** ([HathiTrust "berlin clock"](https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/ls?q1=%22berlin%20clock%22&field1=ocr&a=srchls&ft=ft&lmt=all)). An English source from the late 1980s would have said "the World Clock", "the world time clock" or "the Weltzeituhr". The 1989 *Let's Go Europe* says both Germanys "often meet at the plaza's Weltzeituhr, the international clock", which matches Sanborn's picture of a gathering place but has no compass word near it ([Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/letsgo00harv_1)). So the literal crib BERLINCLOCK is Sanborn's own label. Even if a source passage existed, K4 would be a paraphrase of it, and phrase search cannot catch a paraphrase.

Sanborn's 2014 hint shows he chose the label to point in more than one direction: "There are several really interesting clocks in Berlin" ([Slate, 2014](https://www.slate.com/articles/technology/technology/2014/11/new_kryptos_clue_hint_for_solving_the_cia_s_mysterious_sculpture_puzzle.html)). The community read it as the Mengenlehreuhr until his November 2025 letter fixed it as "the World Clock in Berlin that was the gathering place for the crowds that brought down the Berlin wall" ([Popular Mechanics via Yahoo](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/cia-home-unsolved-puzzle-35-130000082.html)). The physical site offers a bearing without any text. The World Clock stands on a compass-rose (Windrose) mosaic laid in 1969 ([berlin.de](https://www.berlin.de/sehenswuerdigkeiten/3561749-3558930-weltzeituhr.html)), so a visitor, or Sanborn working from photographs, could pair a compass point with the clock unaided. Contemporary press runs against a newspaper source too. The 7 October 1989 protest "began at the clock" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Clock_(Alexanderplatz))), and the 4 November rally filled Alexanderplatz with about 500,000 people ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexanderplatz_demonstration)). Reports of those crowds use place names, and "east-northeast" is a surveyor's or navigator's word that wire copy does not use.

## Everyone who has read K4 treats it as Sanborn's own prose

Sanborn's statements run one way across 35 years, with a single early hedge. In the 1991 ABC footage he kept every option open: "It is either a quotation or it's something I've written, or it's something somebody else has written... It's in plain English" ([1991a transcript](https://thekryptosproject.com/media/transcripts/pdfs/1991a.pdf)). A second 1991 reel, new to the repo, describes how he composed it. **"I probably researched it for 6 months or 8 months before I laid pencil to paper... I thought about the work of the Agency... in context of my own work"**, building from "the things that happened back in the 1970s", and he rejected a "negative message" as "just too easy" ([1991b transcript](https://thekryptosproject.com/media/transcripts/pdfs/1991b.pdf)). That describes composed prose about secrecy, not a passage copied from a book. In 2025 the wording was flat: "The last thing I wrote was on that drive", and "while I was writing the plain text of Kryptos in 1988" ([Popular Mechanics via Yahoo](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/cia-home-unsolved-puzzle-35-130000082.html)).

The dates contain a contradiction worth keeping. The letter dates the writing to 1988, yet credits the fall of the Wall (9 November 1989) as an influence. **The BERLINCLOCK passage was therefore written or revised in late 1989 at the earliest**, which fits the 1990 date of the archive strips and the copper cutting in 1989–90. A source text from 1988 cannot contain the World Clock as the crowds' meeting place, which removes the most natural window for a quotation.

The readers of the recovered text add nothing that points to a source. Kobek said it is "exactly the right length. It makes semantic sense", and also, "There's no way on earth that this is a cryptographic solve" ([Zona Motel](https://zonamotel.substack.com/p/interview-kryptos-k4-uncovered-jarett)). The strips were copies made in 1990 so the CIA historical office could check the text "wasn't offensive", with sentences "cut into strips and taped together out of order" ([RR Auction](https://content.rrauction.com/kryptos-k4-discovered-not-solved-heres-what-actually-happened/)). A text vetted for offence is a statement with content, in more than one sentence. A claim surfaced by search that CIA document 2068965 says Sanborn wrote the text "with a prominent fiction writer" **did not hold up**: the page is an unrelated Eastern Europe intelligence collection ([CIA reading room](https://cia.gov/readingroom/print/2068965)). The steelman for a source rests on two phrases: "selected" in the 1992–93 catalogue ("The words and paragraphs that I selected"), and the 1991 "either a quotation". Both are compatible with K3's way of working, a short adapted passage, but neither has ever been backed by anyone who saw the text.

## Scheidt in 1991 says the method was one designed scheme and the key may not be needed

The new primary source on method is Scheidt's 1991 ABC interview, the earliest on record. He "helped him design the way in which he would encrypt" ("Yes, that's right"). He spent "about a month after we decided on what code we wanted, and then... gave Jim, 'this is how you do it.'" He refused to say whether it is "a common form of encryption". **"It is unique to Jim's design... someone has to discover either the keys or the method"** ([1991c transcript](https://thekryptosproject.com/media/transcripts/pdfs/1991c.pdf)). He also said then that he did not know the message, which conflicts with his 2005 "I know what the message was to be". The 2025 auction lot makes that conflict sharper, since it pairs a signed Scheidt letter with the handwritten plaintext and separately lists "the original K4 coding system" ([RR Auction lot](https://www.rrauction.com/jim-sanborn-kryptos-k4-solution-auction/)). Sanborn's own position in 2025 was "nobody has the method but me" ([AP via MyNorthwest](https://mynorthwest.com/national/solution-goes-on-auction-for-cia-hqs-kryptos-sculpture-that-has-stumped-code-breakers-for-decades/4156053)). The buyer, Paradigm, says it has not read the solution ([Paradigm on X](https://x.com/paradigm/status/2065433682505298063)).

The other method statements are already in the repo: masking, an "intentional" change in method from K1–K3, "more than one stage", matrices and coding charts, and "not a math solution". No public source uses the phrase "two-step process"; treat it as community paraphrase. Read together with the 1991 interview, they describe a hand scheme Scheidt designed for this one piece and Sanborn then altered ("It is a solid system, and I fucked with it", FT 2025). In that scheme, knowing the procedure may be enough without any stated key.

None of these can rule out a paraphrase that never used the literal words, which is exactly how K3 relates to Carter. The vocabulary finding shrinks that risk without removing it: if Sanborn coined BERLINCLOCK, then even a real source would match K4 only in content, and only the sealed archive could confirm it.

## Conclusion

The search changes the project's assumptions more than its experiment list. K4 has no outside plaintext to borrow, so the 24 crib letters and any English-likeness test are the only plaintext evidence the method search will get. A K3-style "find the source, then derive the key" route should drop out of the plan, not stay pending as a hopeful branch. The dates matter too. The BERLINCLOCK wording dates to late 1989 at the earliest, so the K4 string was fixed only months before the copper was cut. That makes a finished 1988 scheme reapplied to a late text, possibly with the hand-made changes Sanborn admits to, more plausible than a long, clean design history. It also counts in favour of candidate methods that tolerate irregularities in the cut ciphertext. Scheidt's 1991 "either the keys or the method" supports the repo's working reading that the procedure, not a hidden key phrase, is what has to be found, and it argues for spending compute on families of procedures rather than on keyword dictionaries.

The remaining channels are worth closing because they are cheap, not because they are likely to pay off. The Google Books manual run takes a quarter of an hour, and the ZEFYS login is free. Together they would turn "untested" into "negative" for the two genres a 1989 source would most likely come from. The Kindle novels and press archives cost money or logins for a low-odds return, so they wait. One archival item would actually change the picture: the AAA finding aid's folder list could show whether Sanborn kept any 1989 Berlin clippings, which is the only evidence short of 2075 that could revive the source-text hypothesis.
