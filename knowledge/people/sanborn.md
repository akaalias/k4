# Jim Sanborn

Herbert James Sanborn Jr. Born November 14, 1945, Washington, DC. Lives and works on
Jimmy Island, Chesapeake Bay, Maryland, with his wife, the sculptor Jae Ko, whose medium
is dyed adding-machine paper; "two fireproof walls" between their studios. No children,
no siblings; "I'm the end of the line." For how he thinks as an encoder see
`../10-sanborn-the-artist.md`; for what he has said see `../02-sanborn-said.md`.

## Family and youth

Only child of two Library of Congress employees. His father, Herbert James Sanborn Sr.,
was a printmaker who directed exhibitions at the Library for thirty years; his mother, a
concert pianist, worked there as a photo researcher. Both were only children. He calls
the federal corridors of his childhood "institutional green hallways" and says they
generated his raw material: "data, science, technology, war, conspiracy, espionage."
As a boy in the 1960s he handled Dead Sea Scrolls through his father's job. He drew
himself as an inventor in a backyard studio. Raised in Alexandria and Arlington; Burgundy
Farm Country Day School, then JEB Stuart High School. Tutored in maths every summer "to
be able to get a D."

## Training

Wanted to be an archaeologist, anthropologist, sociologist or medievalist. Randolph-Macon
College (1968 per Wikipedia, in paleontology, fine arts and social anthropology; 1969 per
his own site, in art history and sociology). A summer archaeology course at Oxford sent
him on digs; "being the large man that he was, he was given the muscle jobs"; found a
human skeleton in Southampton and a forgotten medieval passageway in an Oxford chapel
crypt. Learned to find buried structures by flying over landscape at dawn and dusk when
raking light threw shadows. Tried building a stone structure to understand Romanesque
sculpture. Entered Pratt in 1970 for sculpture and ceramics; "the pottery wheel made him
dizzy"; a representational head was savaged by classmates ("you think you're living in
1940?"), after which he made huge Styrofoam organic forms and "has essentially abandoned
the human form since." MFA 1971. Just before graduating, "his first nervous breakdown,"
anxiety and depression he still manages; left New York and has not set foot there in a
decade.

## Career before Kryptos

Taught at Montgomery College; nine years artist-in-residence at Glen Echo Park. Showed
with Diane Brown in DC and New York; "Jim made art that was too big for any of the
galleries." As an upstart he drove sculptures onto corporate lawns on weekends and
photographed them before security chased him off. Feared Isamu Noguchi would steal his
ideas: "I was paranoid and still am paranoid." NEA fellowships 1982 and 1986; sculpture
symposia in Japan (1983) and Taiwan (1987); Awards in the Visual Arts and Tiffany grants
1988; Pollock-Krasner 1992. The 1980s work made the Earth's invisible forces visible:
lodestones hauled by truck from a Utah mountain in a lightning field that a NASA
physicist pointed him to, arrays of hundreds of compass needles, the Coriolis force,
whirlpools, thunderheads. "I represent a phenomenon, an invisible force, a psychological
feeling, a terror, or something like that."

## The commission

Had applied to GSA programs for about ten years without winning one. In November 1988 a
combined NEA and CIA panel chose him "on the back of his invisible-forces work and the
fact that he was a DC kid," for $250,000, all of which he spent on the piece; "I made
nothing from the project." Considered consulting the Russians or Mossad for the cipher
("probably wouldn't end well"; his contract required him to buy American) and
"reluctantly accepted the CIA's offer to work with Ed Scheidt." Read everything about the
CIA, favourable and hostile. The 1988 Village Voice piece "Art of the State" ("accepting
the money of Satan") "set my career back a good bit." The Northern Virginia Sun predicted
"a festering reservoir of ill will." During installation employees "would point and
laugh, even curse"; a trailer of granite disappeared and the CIA cut him a $5,000 check.
He believed he was followed, his phone tapped, ladders at his windows; "He was getting a
little paranoid" (Sheldon). Built the piece over two and a half years in a warehouse in a
rough part of northeast DC where "he'd found dead bodies in the yard," with a dozen
jigsaws, a thousand blades and nine assistants, David Sheldon chief among them; "the
retention rate was low." Composed the K4 text last, on the long drive back from Holbrook,
Arizona with the petrified tree, and enciphered it on that trip. Showed the scrambled
plaintext to three people in the Office of Historical Intelligence, sent the one with the
photographic memory out of the room, and watched one reader shake and cry. Gave Webster
a sealed envelope at the dedication and says it did not contain the last section.

## After Kryptos

The 1990s turned to "the invisible forces of humans": Covert Obsolescence and the
Archives series from pulped CIA documents obtained in exchange for giving Webster the
keywords; KGB documents from the Library of Congress and a former spy's book, condensed
by a translator and enciphered on etched copper (Archeotranscription, Clandestine Device,
the Deceit, Bias, Light and Shadow Filters); the Code Room cylinder (1993) whose
projected letters "spill out all over the viewers"; the Cyrillic Projector (installed
1997) and Antipodes (1992, 1997). "A Spy in Rome" and Kahn are on the community's reading
list because of him. Mid-1990s: exhausted by clandestine subjects, he went west and
projected perfect cubes and triangles onto buttes from a quarter-mile with a custom
projector, "in order to recall the original crystalline landscape that I envisioned."
Finding himself near Jornada del Muerto on the last trek, he spent the 2000s on the bomb:
Critical Assembly rebuilt the Los Alamos assembly room from objects that "walked up out
of their basements"; Atomic Time at the Corcoran (2003); Terrestrial Physics (2009-10), a
working million-volt accelerator that repeated the Carnegie Institution's 1939 fission
experiment ("I fissioned uranium in my studio"), which Blake Gopnik called "possibly the
most substantial work of art to come out of Washington since the 1950s." The 2010s:
Without Provenance (2018) and Looted? (2020), Khmer antiquities carved by master forgers
in Siem Reap whom he and an archaeologist approached posing as a collector, shown as a
mock auction with fabricated provenance; "an ontological trouble-maker" with "a
prankster's delight." Public cylinders of projected multilingual text on campuses (Lux,
Lingua, A Comma A, Radiance, Iacto), "Kryptos twins rolled into upright burritos" that
"tend to get filled with empty beer cans." Coastline (1993), a wave pool driven by live
ocean data, is his own favourite after Kryptos. Indian Run Park (1994) seeded with
thousands of arrowheads for visitors to find. The 2020s: Euclid and Descartes in bronze;
reading prehistory; "something Paleolithic" next.

## Health, money, secrecy

Treated for metastatic cancer about a decade before 2025, during which he hastily
gathered his papers and mistakenly included the K4 scraps. "I can't feel anything from
my navel down"; back and legs fail him; "very much" immunocompromised; ozone-boxed his
groceries through the pandemic. He and Ko are "cash-poor," having put everything into
real estate. Death threats, white-powder envelopes, three attempted home invasions, a
visitor on the island, a shotgun above the bed, panic buttons. Tens of thousands of
correspondents in 35 years; a dozen submissions a day by 2025; "The thing about AI is
it's lying." He suspected Dunin of being "a spy in her own right"; she was once tempted
to grab his briefcase of Kryptos notes and run, and didn't, suspecting a test. "Secrecy
is power, that's the unfortunate reality. And I have been resented for having this
secret." Guardianship was "satisfaction" and "servitude." Decided "several years" before
2025 to auction the secret; sold the archive on his 80th birthday for $962,500, netting
about $770,000, part pledged to programs for the disabled.

## Self-descriptions worth keeping

"I'm basically a science pragmatist." "In some ways I'm a fiction writer." "I'm not good
at [keeping secrets]." "Retaining an aura of mystery is, I think, good for any art." "This
is a piece that requires two artists: encoder and decoder." "Creative individuals often
have problems. We all have the same problems."
