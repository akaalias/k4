# 03. What Scheidt has said that bears on K4

Edward Michael Scheidt, born July 20, 1939. Army signals intelligence from 1957, CIA
Office of Communications from 1963, 26 years, twelve overseas as a field communications
officer under State Department cover (Vientiane, Damascus, Tel Aviv, Manila, Athens),
"mostly used one-time pad paper encryption systems," chairman of the CIA's Cryptographic
Center, retired December 1989. Co-founded TecSec in 1990. Roughly 36 patents, almost all
on Constructive Key Management: a working key built on the client by combining several
secret "splits" through a combiner, with access labels riding inside the encrypted
object. Vice chair of ANSI X9F and convenor of an ISO working group on digital currency as
of 2023. Alive as far as the record shows; silent on the 2025-26 events.

Chronological. Verbatim unless marked.

## 1999, Washington Post (July 19)

"I could use methods to encrypt it that had a historic basis, that didn't compromise any
current methods." He and Sanborn wanted "to make something that could eventually be
deciphered or extracted, rather than something that will never be done, ever." He figured
the first chunks would stand a few years, "the last part, perhaps ten." "The last 97
characters? Don't hold your breath." "I saved the best for last." "No clues, sorry." To
Stein after his briefing: "the solution is correct, but you didn't do it the way I intended
you to do it." (Stein had used frequency analysis without recovering the keywords first.)

## 2000, Baltimore Sun (March 17)

"Its intent was to last roughly five years; it was modeled along those lines." "There was
a change in the methodology. It was intentional." The riddle's answer "addresses the
painstaking work of gathering and analyzing intelligence. In this veiled world, what seems
apparent can be a distortion and the truth is learned by peeling back the layers."

## 2003, Dunin's road trip (secondhand, Dunin and Warzin via Wilson)

He "did more than simply teach Sanborn various encryption systems, but did the actual
encryption and design of the Kryptos code" (contradicted by Sanborn). "It was necessary to
recover the keys, and the keys were concealed at the sculpture." He "used to be an 'op',
and part 4 is designed to be solved in a straight-forward manner with pencil and paper,
and that he did things to 'disguise the English' in part 4, so that it couldn't be solved
in the frequency analysis ways that were used for parts 1-3." He and Sanborn "debated over
the use of obscure languages and decided against that." "Parts 1-3 had been solved
without recovering the key first." He "implied that he had built in some dead-ends to
throw some people off the track." "Part of the code was designed to be solved with
multiple keys, a code where different keys could be given to different people, but they'd
all still be able to solve it." "Imagine that you have an operative in place that you will
need to get a message to in the future. It could be 15 years down the road... You have to
assume that they will not remember the 'key'. How do you deliver a message in such a way
that it conveys not only the encrypted data, but also the key?" "In a modern digital
system, the 'key' is the keyword or number... In analog systems (as used in Kryptos) the
'key' is the algorithm." He implied that even those who read K1-K3 "still didn't
understand how the message was constructed and how his fictitious 'operative in the field'
would have solved the problem." "A code that could be solved without giving away who'd
solved it." His office had Kryptos photos and Egyptian artwork.

## 2004, a cryptography talk (September, confirmed in part by Scheidt)

"I used a bit of stego when designing the fourth part of Kryptos." "In the first 3 parts, I
gave anyone attempting to break the code the advantage of the English language with all
its known patterns, but I removed that advantage in the fourth part." His own
clarification: "I did say that in the last part the advantage of the language was taken
away. I also stated that there was a piece relating to Stego, but did not state how or in
what context."

## 2005, Wired (January 21, Zetter)

"I provided the cryptographic process as well as worked with him with what he was looking
to do as far as the story. We came up with a methodology using some of the known
cryptographic solutions (at the time)." "There are four different processes. Two of them
are similar and the other two are different things. The first three processes were
designed so that a person could, through cryptographic analysis, have access to the
English language. And the last process, I masked the English language so it's more of a
challenge now. It's progressively harder in the challenges." "All four are done in the
English language. The message could have been in another language. But this particular
puzzle is in the English language... You can get insight into the sculpture through that
technique [frequency counting] because the English language is still visible through the
code. But with this other technique in the fourth part, I disguise that. So... you need to
solve the technique first and then go for the puzzle." Are all four processes known
processes? "The masking technique may not be known." On Sanborn masking further: "I can't
respond to that one. I haven't heard him say that before. It's possible I guess. I haven't
talked to Jim on what he did, but I do know... there was some masking techniques that were
used and that's about it." "I know what the message was to be. But since he's the one who
had the chisel in his hands, there could be some changes." "The idea of encoding a message
is not only to encode the externals of a message (the English language), but the message
itself. Once it is readable, it may have other encoding that's involved in it... We may
establish a code that 1 o'clock on Friday is equal to 'cake.'" Could someone translate the
message but not know what it means? "That's right. And that's where the masking and all
these other kinds of techniques can come into play." "Would you approach it mathematically?
Would you approach it in the context of secret writings or symbols?" "I think there are 98
characters left." "I can only say the intent was to have a larger meaning." "I'm not aware
of anything being buried, no."

## 2005, NPR (June 9)

"If you could determine that, the number of E's vs. the number of Z's in the English
language, then you would have some insight into the potential words." Reporter: "in part
four he deliberately masked that advantage." Scheidt "insists even he doesn't know exactly
how it turned out, that though he helped with the basic structure of the code, he's never
tried to crack Sanborn's final message."

## 2009, Wired (Levy)

"I was reminded of my need to preserve the agency's secrets. You know, don't tell him the
current way of doing business. And don't create something that you cannot break, but at
the same time, make it something that will last a while." The two "experimented... with a
range of techniques, including poly-alphabetic substitution, shifting matrices, and
transposition" (Levy). "We had to play a little on the clandestine side." "There may be
more to the puzzle than what you see. Just because you broke it doesn't mean you have the
answer." "The intelligence picture includes mirrors and obfuscation."

## 2011, Kryptos dinner, Zola (secondhand, Hannon via Bean)

K4 "is not mathematical (although this does not preclude it being modeled mathematically),
it is simple, can be remembered, and executed years later when used with the correct key
word/s." "K4 cryptography is similar to what would be provided agents or pilots in case of
capture." Asked whether K4 was a one-off, he "looked to Jim and said 'well Jim, how are we
going to answer that one?' To which Jim simply replied 'we don't answer it'." Reminded by
Dunin that he once said Kryptos "uses a system unknown to anyone on the planet," he "stated
he didn't recall that conversation."

## 2014, New York Times (November 20)

He "would not have expected to find people still banging their heads against Kryptos so
many years later." "No, not really. But a technique that I used obviously worked."

## 2015, Kryptos workshop (October 24, transcribed by Bean)

"Some of the things that you see or you should have seen is the impact of some kind of
math function on the English language." "The tools that you have been using or would be
using are tools based on periodicity of letters... and so there's a mask of some type now,
now you don't have that as your initial tool... you've gotta remember your process as well
as what you're trying to do." "Added to that is the math associated with the cryptography
and that's some substitution transposition... but you carry still you're carrying the
weight of the language when you're doing that. And the other intent was is there a way to
make the challenge so that the first step is that thing and now that gets to be hard."
"The intent was and still is if I had to communicate to any of you in a foreign country, a
phone call away and I wanted to do it over a period of time but I don't know when... I
have to do something that you could come back years later and recreate." "What happens if
I put a gun to his head, and now he wants to tell me there's a gun there without getting
himself shot, I know how to do that... I used to make those codes, for captured people."
"The human side of this type of thing is a much higher percentage." "The fun part is to see
how the folks approach the problem... half the problem is how do you approach the
problem." "This is analogue [hits paper]." "The K4 may not be something as such so that's
sort of unique in that sense but the others have all the intrinsics of classical
cryptographic puzzles." "It lends itself to building layers or you could mix and match
which is a certain amount of that took place." "The thing that I had thought of and I was
asked not to do... this is based on the English language... I could have moved to a
different math base, in other words this is base 2 we could move to base 16 and then
evolve the problem... different folks thought that's not fair Scheidt." "You know the Mayan
language and you know how to handle base 60 then you've figured out the answer. Now taking
that and... adding transposition and some of these other techniques it would give you
another headache." "I believe the human uses a base number." On checking K4: "I honestly
haven't gone back in many many years"; "once, a couple of times"; then "never... I stayed
away"; "the 97, I intentionally did not do this stay away because if Jim was going to do
something then OK Jim does something and then I can honestly say well I only know what I
did." Sanborn: "so if Jim made an encoding error you would not have known that he did so?"
Scheidt: "no." "He produced the plaintext, that was part of his contract... it reads like
it's disjointed but there's a story that goes with it." "Is there any other level of
secret I can have with him that only him knows... I could put the word coca-cola in a text
which was encrypted and then you decrypt it and now if coca cola took a meaning a dual
meaning." "Except for K4 I didn't. I did all the others." "We agreed a long time ago it
really is Jim's project, I'm sort of the back person that says 2 and 2 is 4 not 5." "The
intent was to create a secret and then the secret could have different levels of being
able to break or different kinds of math." "This is not a one-time system, the difficulty
would be forever." On executing it: pencil and paper. "I've had others say you definitely
have a convoluted mind." "What's a period, because there's more than one sentence in there
and how do you characterise that and do we have apostrophes, special characters... special
characters don't work, you have to take that into account." Is K4 one-stage like K1-K3?
"No, I would consider it more than one stage." "There's a masking technique that applied
so that the English language wouldn't be available." Which systems did Sanborn use? "I
just don't remember how he selected... at the time, I believe it was [his selection]."
"So you showed him the how and he did the what?" "Right." Same system twice? "I can't
answer the question directly, because that's a Jim's call." Masking first or encryption
first? No clear answer. The transcript notes Sanborn "has said that something that was key
is no longer there."

## 2020, A.J. Jacobs interview (transcript on groups.io, quoted by Bean)

"If you can change the language base then it becomes in my favor and not your favor of
trying to break it. It becomes more of a challenge now, when it was used as the mask it
was current."

## 2020, New York Times (January 29, by email)

The fourth section, "just 97 characters," "could, in itself, present a decryption challenge." The last passage "uses
what is known as a masking technique, a further level of obfuscation" (Schwartz's paraphrase around the quote;
`sources/nyt-2020-northeast-clue.txt`).

## 2022, Northern Virginia Magazine (October 14)

"We used voice as the medium." They met at secret locations, wrote very little down, left
devices behind. "The concern for eavesdropping was real." K1-K3 "were designed not to last
a long time." With K4 the partners wanted "a real challenge." He had asked to use an
extinct language: "That gives me an advantage." "They said no." "There's an education
element to it." Has he tried to solve K4? "Never tempted." He is "a code creator, not a
code breaker." The writer notes Sanborn recalled the scheme from memory, "which may
explain a few minor errors."

## Reading these together

Five things never change across 23 years: the English in K4 was masked; the mask is a
technique to be solved first; K4 is more than one stage; the system is simple, memorable,
hand-executable and keyword-driven, like a duress or capture code for an agent; and he
never checked Sanborn's final K4. His own professional habit is keys assembled from parts
with a combiner. Phrases often attributed to him that no source supports: "you'll kick
yourself," "it's a matrix," "the method is 1990s."
