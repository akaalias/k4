# Scheidt's own patent outranks the field manuals

The single best new hypothesis source for K4 is not a field manual but Scheidt's own patent **US 6,075,865** (with Wack, priority 1998), which writes down a letter-for-letter "text/key relation" made of keyed shifts sandwiched around keyed permutation tables, mod W. At W = 26 it contains the K1/K2 Kryptos tableau as a special case, and its general form, two key streams on either side of a keyed alphabet, is outside everything the project has closed. Behind it come three documented "matrix, then table" agent systems that keep one ciphertext letter per plaintext letter on 26 letters: the CIA's own 1970s Polish agent system (a running key written into a box and read out by columns before the tableau), the Czech exile Chiffre XIII (a keyword-shaped partial-row transposition, then a period-5 shifted mixed alphabet), and the keyword-disrupted and triangle transpositions (Czech EVA, Rijmenants). Friedman's training texts add a fourth group, rules for *when* the key letter advances, but checks run while writing this report show that the keyword-only versions of those rules all die on the standard and KRYPTOS tables. They survive only with free alphabets, where the project's existing deciders already fall to the shuffled-copy rate. Most of the rest is either covered (hesitation and prime stepping, autokey, combined key splits, double passes, deliberate errors), digit-producing (TAPIR, VIC, MARTA, DRYAD), or tied to carried material that cannot be memorised (RS 44 stencils, SOE WOKs and LOPs). Several primary texts that would settle open details, among them *Elementary Military Cryptography*, TM 32-220, NSA's *German Agent Systems*, and Bury's *Cryptologia* paper, could not be fetched automatically and are listed at the end.

## Scheidt wrote down a stepped tableau chain in 1998

Of roughly 33 granted US patents naming Edward M. Scheidt, almost all describe Constructive Key Management, in which a working key is assembled from several "splits" that are never transmitted ([Google Patents US6608901](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6608901)). One patent stands apart. **US 6,075,865** and its continuation **US 6,266,417**, "Cryptographic communication process and apparatus," define a per-symbol cipher: "O_t = α_N(t) + π_N[α_N-1(t) + π_N-1[... + π_1[I_t + α_0(t)]] ...], mod W, where α_N ... α_0 are N+1 additive transformations defined by the encrypt key, where π_N ... are N permutations" ([US6075865A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6075865A/en)). In plain words, the input symbol is shifted, looked up in a keyed table, shifted again, looked up in another table, and so on. The key chooses the tables from a stored set and sets each additive's starting point. The additives then step "one place every R times... R is preferably a large prime number," or with a "hesitation" that skips every L-th step, or "according to the value of a previous output" ([US6075865A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6075865A/en)). The patent's worked example is a machine design (W = 256, eight tables from a stored 25, a 21-symbol key, an IFF application), and its block-cipher mode, which uses a four-stage feedback register over 16 rounds, does not keep letter-for-letter correspondence.

The per-symbol mode at W = 26 can be worked by hand. With π_1 = the inverse of the KRYPTOS alphabet, π_2 = the KRYPTOS alphabet, the outer additives zero and α_1 the keyword, the formula reduces to c = π[π⁻¹(p) + k_t], which is exactly the Quagmire III method of K1 and K2. That reduction is this report's derivation, not a claim in the patent. Read this way, the patent is a written generalisation of the Kryptos cipher by its designer, and it offers three directions of variation: more layers of tables (one reading of Sanborn's "double matrixes and triple matrixes... then went back to this table"), shifts on both sides of a table, and shifts that move on a prime count, hesitate, or feed back from the previous output. The patent's 1998 priority date is eight years after Kryptos, so it shows how Scheidt habitually framed a text/key relation, not what he used in 1989.

Most of these variations are already covered. Value-dependent stepping is ciphertext autokey through a keyed table, closed at every lag (experiments 0002, 0025, 0064, 0118). Prime-count stepping is the shift that changes every m letters of 0088. Hesitation belongs to the skip and stutter schedules of 0127 and 0128, which found no setting on the standard or KRYPTOS tables. Adding two keyword splits mod 26 is the closed two-part-key family (F3: 0014, 0043, 0059, 0115). Two passes through two vocabulary tables is 0043 and 0059. The variation that is genuinely new is the **sandwich**: c = π₂[π₁[p + a_t] + b_t], with two independent key streams a and b on either side of a fixed keyed alphabet. With a constant, a can be absorbed into π₁ and the result is Quagmire IV (F1.2). With a periodic, the alphabet in force changes with a's phase, which no repeating key on one table reproduces. With π₁ and π₂ drawn from {standard, KRYPTOS, their inverses} and periods up to about 13 each, the 24 known letters give an exact test by backtracking over a and propagating to b. This fits the open leaf **F3.1, combiners other than addition**.

The key-split patents also contain a structural echo of Kryptos. Their labels let one object be read by holders of different splits ([US6490680B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6490680B1/en)), which matches the secondhand report that "different keys could be given to different people". Scheidt's earliest patent, **US 5,432,851** (filed 1993), is a manual challenge-and-response token ([US5432851A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5432851A/en)), the same shape as the DRYAD pilot authentication tables. Neither yields a new letter cipher.

## Friedman's key-advance rules die on the fixed tables

The Friedman training texts the project had not mined describe a family of aperiodic tableau procedures in which the table stays fixed and only the moment the key letter advances changes ([MC III](https://bribes.org/crypto/Military_Cryptanalysis_Friedman_3.pdf)). The variants are: the key letter changes with each plaintext word (§6); groups follow a 1-2-3-4-5 pattern (§3b); each key letter's own value sets how many letters it covers, so PREPARE gives runs of P = 16, R = 18, and so on (§16b); the key restarts after a chosen plaintext or ciphertext "interruptor" letter (§19, §21); a short keyword is expanded into a long key by taking sections that end at the letters numbered 1, 2, 3 in turn, so CHRISTMAS grows from 9 letters to 45 (§35c); or the digits of a fraction such as 1/49 set the run lengths (§35d; [TM 11-485 §44e](https://WWW.radionerds.com/images/b/bb/TM_11-485.pdf)). Every one of them keeps letter-for-letter correspondence, needs only a keyword and a remembered rule, and fits "simple, can be remembered... with the correct key word/s, not mathematical" as well as anything in the literature.

The known letters dispose of most of this group cheaply. Under any schedule, two places that share an alphabet must agree: the same plaintext letter must give the same carved letter, and different plaintext letters must give different carved letters. Three adjacent pairs break that rule whatever the alphabets. N and O at 26-27 both sit under Q, T and S at 33-34 both under S, and I and N at 68-69 both under T. So **any schedule that keeps one alphabet across 26-27, 33-34 or 68-69 is dead without a search** (computed here from the carved text and the cribs). That kills per-word keying (§6) outright, since NORTH and EAST each contain one of the pairs. For the keyword-only schedules, the shift is fixed by the key letter itself, so each dictionary word gives one deterministic test. Checks run for this report over 227,485 words from the system word list, on the standard and KRYPTOS tables in Vigenère, Beaufort and variant forms, found **no word that fits all 24 known letters** for value-as-run-length (A = 1, and A = 0 with zero-length skips) or for the CHRISTMAS expansion. The ciphertext-interruptor schedule depends only on the carved text, the interruptor letter and the keyword length. All 26 letters × lengths 1-26 were tested with the key letters left free, and none fits on either fixed table. With an independent free alphabet per slot, about a third of those settings survive, the same uninformative regime that 0127 and 0128 recorded for skip and stutter.

These checks were run outside the harness, in a scratch script, without shuffled-copy controls, so they are leads to be formalised as an F1.4 follow-up, not closed leaves. What remains open in this group is narrow: the fraction-digit schedules (keyword plus a small denominator), the plaintext-interruptor schedules (whose break points depend on unknown plaintext outside the cribs), and every schedule on free alphabets, which should go through 0119's and 0123's deciders as new partitions of the 24 places. Two more Friedman items are worth a line each. The twelve ways of reading an equivalent off two sliding components ([MC II §7, App. 1](https://bribes.org/crypto/Military_Cryptanalysis_Friedman_2.pdf)) reduce to Quagmire forms with Beaufort variants once key values are free, which F1.1 and F1.2 already decide. They matter only when the key is a fixed word, where the index letter relabels it, so they belong in any dictionary-key test as 12 × 26 conventions. Double encipherment through two different mixed tables ([TM 11-485 §49](https://WWW.radionerds.com/images/b/bb/TM_11-485.pdf)) is the closed two-pass family, unless the second table is free.

## The CIA's Polish agent system transposes the key, not the text

The documented agent system closest to Kryptos is American. Jan Bury's account of CIA agent ciphers in 1970s Poland, as summarised on the former GDR cipher service's archive site, describes this procedure. The agent takes a verse from a book at page = day-of-year + 10 and line = day of month, writes it row by row into a 10 × 10 box, reads it out column by column, and combines the result with the text through a 26 × 26 reciprocal table ([scz.bplaced.net §9.2](https://scz.bplaced.net/m.html#uCIA-pol), citing Bury, *Cryptologia* 2007, [dblp](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/pid/89/206.html)). The printed table is Beaufort-type (p + k + c = 25 mod 26). The agent memorises a rule rather than a key, uses a matrix, then "goes back to the table", which is very nearly Sanborn's description. It differs from the closed running-key tests because the key text is transposed before it is used. Friedman documents a keyword-only cousin, in which a keyword's numerical order is used as a columnar key on a rectangle holding the normal alphabet repeated an agreed number of times, and the column read becomes the keystream ([MC III §35b](https://bribes.org/crypto/Military_Cryptanalysis_Friedman_3.pdf)).

A first pass was run for this report. K1-K3 plaintexts (each, and concatenated) were tested as the key text, in blocks of 97 or 100 letters at every offset, written into boxes of width 2-50, read by columns top-down or bottom-up, on the standard and KRYPTOS tables in three forms: 1,388,268 settings, **best 7 of 24 known letters**. That is the chance level for that many trials (the project's earlier running-key sweeps reached 9 of 24 on scrambled copies). This closes only the cheapest corner. Still open are keyword-ordered column reads, the Carter text and Sanborn's letters as key texts, a free alphabet in place of the fixed table, and Friedman's repeated-alphabet box keyed by dictionary words, which needs only a keyword and a repetition count.

## Czech exile and disrupted transpositions fill the "matrix" gap

Three documented hand transpositions keep letters one for one, are keyed by a word, and are absent from the project's move library. Rijmenants's keyword-disrupted columnar transposition fills each row only up to the column of the next keyword digit and completes the empty cells afterwards. In the triangle variant, each triangular area "starts at the first keyword position and goes one step further to the right with each lower row" ([Rijmenants, Hand Ciphers](https://ciphermachinesandcryptology.com/en/handciphers.htm)). The Czech London exile system **EVA** (type TT, letters only) writes the text into a triangle that grows by two cells per row, with height n ≥ √k and keyword length 2n − 1, reads it out by columns, writes it into a second table under the same numbering, and reads it again ([scz.bplaced.net §10.1](https://scz.bplaced.net/m.html#eva)). For 97 letters that gives n = 10, a 19-letter keyword and three empty cells.

**Chiffre XIII** (type TS) is the most K4-shaped of the Czech catalogue. It uses a memorised sentence rotated by the date, a five-row transposition table in which rows 1, 3 and 5 are full width, rows 2 and 4 are partial and row 4 is cyclically shifted, columns read in the keyword's alphabetical order, and then a substitution "again with the keyword" through a five-row tableau of one mixed alphabet, each row shifted one place ([scz.bplaced.net §10.1](https://scz.bplaced.net/m.html#roem13)). Checking the published example shows the five rows used cyclically, letter by letter. That makes the substitution a period-5 progressive Quagmire on 26 letters, applied after a transposition. Period 5 after every built move is already dead on free alphabets (F6.1, F6.2), so the only new element is the keyword-shaped mask. That makes it a cheap, exact test: dictionary keywords of 24 letters or more, or sentences, define the mask, and 0119's decider handles the period-5 table.

The other "matrix" systems rank lower. The Nihilist transposition permutes the rows and columns of a full square by one key ([ACA](https://www.cryptogram.org/downloads/aca.info/ciphers/NihilistTransposition.pdf)). Because 97 is prime, it needs a 10 × 10 square with three blanks, which pushes it into F6.4's padding territory; a single key for both axes is only 10! ≈ 3.6 million orders. **RS 44** is a true 24 × 25 stencil grid with ten white cells per row, but the stencil was carried and changed daily ([Rijmenants, RS 44](https://ciphermachinesandcryptology.com/en/rasterschlussel44.htm)). It cannot be "remembered with the key word" unless the mask is derived from a keyword, which is what Chiffre XIII and the disrupted transpositions already supply. The Stasi and Soviet "Doppelwürfel" (a memorised verse plus a shared keyword feeding two key boxes) is structurally "double matrixes, then the table", but it emits digits ([Vreisleben](http://www.signalshed.com/docs/stasi.pdf); [scz.bplaced.net §10.2](https://scz.bplaced.net/m.html)). Its letter-only reduction is the double keyword transposition already closed in F6 (0112) and F11.2. The East German TAPIR and KOBRA, VIC, Sorge's SUBWAY checkerboard, the Rote Kapelle book additive, Czech TTS, MARTA and Růžena, and the Mossad syllable table are all digit systems, as are the US tactical DRYAD and BATCO-type sheets ([DRYAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DRYAD); [BATCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BATCO)). They are conditionally excluded. Doppelkasten is digraphic on 25 letters and dies under 0052. The US Special Forces DIANA pad is a reciprocal Beaufort table on a random pad ([sfrvn.net](https://sfrvn.net/c1/diana.asp)), as are SOE LOPs and the BND letter "worm" ([scz.bplaced.net §3.11.4](https://scz.bplaced.net/m.html)). With a known text as the pad they become the closed running-key family; with a random pad they are untestable.

## Masking meant Friedman's frequency disguise, and most of it changes length

No official COMSEC definition of plaintext "masking" turned up. NSA's own uses are TEMPEST noise and traffic-flow padding ([Boak, Vol. I](https://www.archives.gov/files/declassification/iscap/pdf/2009-049-doc1.pdf)), and neither fits. Scheidt's phrasing matches Friedman's: variants exist for "masking the characteristic frequencies," nulls give "the masking of the cipher units," and a "basic period would be distorted or masked" by aperiodic repetitions ([MC I pp. 68-69, 101-102](https://bribes.org/crypto/Military_Cryptanalysis_Friedman_1.pdf)). Letter-only homophones in Friedman's example double the length or more, so they cannot produce 97 letters from 97. The one length-keeping, hand-simple frequency mask in the manuals is **free choice of cipher row**: FM 24-5 tells the M-94 user to "select one of the cipher text rows at random" and to avoid habitual rows ([FM 24-5, par. 59](https://WWW.radionerds.com/images/1/14/FM_24-5.pdf)). On a tableau, a free choice among all 26 rows imposes no constraint at all, but a choice restricted to the rows named by a keyword's letters can be tested exactly. The 24 known letters need **at least 12 distinct key values** in every reading on the standard and KRYPTOS tables (12 in standard Beaufort, 14-16 in the others; computed here). So any free choice among rows named by KRYPTOS, PALIMPSEST or ABSCISSA (seven to nine distinct letters) is dead, and the idea survives only with row sets of 12 or more.

The documented plaintext-preparation rules mostly concern the scorer, not the cipher. "Russian copulation" (cut the message and send the second part first) was standard US practice before the KL-7 ([Boak p. 37](https://www.archives.gov/files/declassification/iscap/pdf/2009-049-doc1.pdf)) and Soviet VIC practice ([VIC cipher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VIC_cipher)). Because the clue positions refer to the enciphered order, a cut is invisible to every crib test. It matters only to an English scorer, which should read the decrypt cyclically, from 97 back to 1. FM 24-5 adds spelled punctuation (STOP, X), digits spelled one by one (40 = FOURZERO), letter-per-digit substitution, and the addressee and signature placed at random inside the body ([FM 24-5, pars. 27, 60](https://WWW.radionerds.com/images/1/14/FM_24-5.pdf)). These belong in issue #11's scorer tolerances. SOE's deliberate errors at agreed places ([Englandspiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Englandspiel)) are the documented precedent closest to Scheidt's "I used to make those codes, for captured people" and to K1-K3's misspellings. At the cipher level they are the one-wrong-letter tests the project has already run (0031, 0068, 0093, 0094). Their value is interpretive: a K4 decrypt with one odd letter would be expected, not suspect.

## Conclusion

The search changes where Scheidt's hand should be looked for. The field-manual literature mostly confirms that the project's closed families were the right ones, and its most distinctive hand idea, rules for when the key advances, fails on the known letters as soon as the tables are fixed, because three adjacent crib pairs (26-27, 33-34, 68-69) forbid any shared alphabet. What survives is structural rather than schedular: a second key stream on the far side of the keyed alphabet, a key that is itself transposed in a box, or a keyword-shaped transposition that the move library has never contained. Each of these matches a sentence Scheidt or Sanborn actually said better than the closed families do, and each is decided exactly by machinery the project already has.

The caveat cuts the other way too. Every surviving family that frees an alphabet lands in the regime where the 24 letters stop deciding and F1.5's climb has weak power. So these new families earn their place only in their fixed-table and keyword-only forms. If those die, the honest conclusion is that the documented hand repertoire Scheidt drew on is close to exhausted at the crib level, and that what remains is either a free-alphabet cell waiting on a better English decider or a mechanism no surviving manual describes.
