# Ed Scheidt

Edward Michael Scheidt (sources vary on "Micheal"). Born July 20, 1939, San Bernardino
County, California. Parents Edward Henry Scheidt (1892-1976), a government employee, and
Ruth Christine Adema (1911-2001), an AT&T switchboard operator. Married Mary Jane Shope,
July 1974. Charter Grand Knight of a Knights of Columbus council; conceived a chalice
blessed by John Paul II in 2001; assistant scoutmaster; Boy Scouts council board member.
For what he has said about K4 see `../03-scheidt-said.md`.

## Career

Cor Jesu High School, New Orleans, class of 1957; he later said Kryptos "took me back to
the math days of Cor Jesu." Army Signals Intelligence from 1957, with the Armed Forces
Security Agency lineage that became NSA. Hired by the CIA in 1963 as a communications
officer in the Office of Communications. Twenty-six years, twelve overseas under State
Department Foreign Service cover: Vientiane from 1963, Damascus and Tel Aviv 1966-68,
Manila 1971-73, Athens 1978-80; promoted through the Foreign Service grades on paper.
"Mostly used one-time pad paper encryption systems." BA in business administration,
University of Maryland, 1970; MS in telecommunications, George Washington, 1975. Rose to
chairman of the CIA's Cryptographic Center (a title whose organization no public source
describes); Webster called him "The Wizard of Codes"; Bill Gertz coined "Deep Throat of
Codes" in the Washington Times in 1991. Retired December 1989 (1988 per one 2022 piece;
1990 per Dunin's FAQ) and was "loaned" back to the project.

## TecSec

Co-founded TecSec in Vienna, Virginia in 1990; built the first portable-satellite STU-III
secure phone in his basement; about 500 in Foreign Service use by 2002. Three decades on
Constructive Key Management: keys never transmitted but constructed on the client by
combining multiple secret "splits" (domain, role or label, random) through a combiner,
with access rules embedded in the encrypted object; "while only transferring benign
information between the participants" (FIPS 140-2 policy, 2006). Became ANSI X9.69 and
X9.73. Roughly 36 patents 1993-2015: the key-split combiner family (US6608901,
US7079653, US7095851, US7095852, US8712046), multiple-level access, biometric units,
container security, later blockchain identity and a 2025 publication on cryptographic
communication binding. TecSec sued IBM, Adobe and others in 2010; the Adobe case ended in
2019 with zero damages. Co-wrote a 2004 Pentagon report, "Information-Centric Security."
Spoke at Bouchercon 2001. Vice chair of ANSI X9F and convenor of the ISO working group on
digital-currency security as of 2023, at 83-84; working on "crypto with currency."

## Kryptos

Spent about four months in 1989 teaching Sanborn pencil-and-paper systems "from the late
1800s through WWII" and designing "the processes," at secret locations, by voice, with
devices left behind, moving occasionally, because "the media had listening devices." His
brief to himself: "don't tell him the current way of doing business. And don't create
something that you cannot break, but at the same time, make it something that will last a
while." Wanted an extinct language and was refused ("not fair"). Showed Sanborn "the
how"; Sanborn chose "the what." Verified K1-K3 during development "to check that the
math worked" and deliberately never checked K4: "if Jim was going to do something then OK
Jim does something and then I can honestly say well I only know what I did." Would not
have known of an encoding error. Expected the whole puzzle solved in five, seven or ten
years. Told Stein in 1998 "the solution is correct, but you didn't do it the way I
intended." Has "never" been tempted to attack K4: "a code creator, not a code breaker."
Denied any role in the Cyrillic Projector.

## Character, as interviewers saw him

"A quiet, professorial man who favors ties with hieroglyphic patterns" (Post, 1999);
"matter-of-fact and low-key," interviewed over a steak-and-cheese at a longtime CIA
hangout, does no crosswords or Wordle, "seldom associated with Kryptos because he prefers
anonymity... But I really don't advertise" (Northern Virginia Magazine, 2022). Office
walls: Kryptos photos, one signed by Webster, and Egyptian art. Deflects with "that's a
Jim's call," "No clues, sorry," "Don't hold your breath," "I saved the best for last."
"I've had others say you definitely have a convoluted mind." "It's a serious business
playing spy." "The fun part is to see how the folks approach the problem." Enjoys the
solver's process more than any solution. "Remember, we try not to deceive you." Loyal
to Sanborn's authority over the artwork and to the agency's secrets, and uses both as
shields. Silent in public on the 2025 auction, the Smithsonian find, Paradigm and K5.

## Why he matters to the method

His operational life was manual field cryptography for officers who had to remember a
system and execute it years later, under stress, possibly under duress, and signal
duress without being shot. His professional vocabulary is keys built from parts with a
combiner and "benign" transferred information. His K4 statements match: a mask on the
English, more than one stage, simple, memorable, keyword-driven, "the key is the
algorithm," "the keys were concealed at the sculpture," "dead-ends," and the possibility
of "a dual meaning" code inside readable text.
