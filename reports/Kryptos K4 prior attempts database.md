# Public sweeps have closed K4's simple doors

**Thirty-six years of public work on Kryptos K4 have ruled out almost every cipher a 24-letter crib can test, and found nothing.** The record has two layers. The thin formal layer is two refereed papers: Bauer, Link and Molle's Hill-cipher search (2016) and Bean's cryptodiagnosis and Gromark search (2021). The far larger informal layer is mostly a 2025–2026 wave of AI-assisted GitHub repositories (keithadler, Sovi11, Zekios, nitsuah, KryptosBot), which publish exact crib-consistency proofs over stated parameter ranges. Between them, the following are now excluded or exhausted, all with zero survivors beyond chance: pure transposition, monoalphabetic and homophonic substitution, any cipher that cannot map a letter to itself, 25-letter fractionation, periodic keys of length 1 to 26 under all eight KRYPTOS-tableau conventions, Hill matrices up to 4×4, Gromark over every cipher alphabet, trifid and bifid with unknown squares, the K1–K3 texts and large English corpora as running keys, columnar transposition in every column order at widths 2 to 14 combined with a periodic key, and **every single or double K3-style grid rotation at every width pair from 2 to 96, combined with a repeating tableau key**. The method itself is still undisclosed. In September 2025 two journalists recovered the plaintext from Sanborn's archive, but neither they nor Sanborn, the auction house or the 2026 buyer Paradigm has released anything about how K4 was enciphered. What is left open is what the cribs cannot decide on their own: keys longer than 26, two unknown alphabets at certain periods, three or more stages, interior nulls and hand padding conventions, wide or irregular transpositions, and keys from unknown texts. For this project's two favoured directions, the cheapest real gaps are a K3 rotation with hand-style padding paired with plaintext autokey or two different tableau keys, and keyed double transposition with one width of 7. On the statistical dispute: experiment 0056 and Sovi11 measured different things. 0056's method is the sound one, but its headline figure for the doublet column is about five times too strong. The "slightly different tableau" remark does have a primary source. It concerns K1 and K2, and it does not change the alphabet.

## Two refereed papers and a 2026 code wave make up the record

Before 2020, K4 work was mostly unbounded. Jim Gillogly reported "on the order of 20 billion trial decryptions spread over two dozen different systems" in 1999 but never listed the systems ([Gillogly 1999](http://www.elonka.com/kryptos/mirrors/cypherpunks/1999/0930.html)). An NSA team spent one day on K4 in 1992–93, and an NSA analyst tried autokey around 2007; neither published bounds ([NSA summary](https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/cia-kryptos-sculpture/KRYPTOS_Summary.pdf); [Black Vault](https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/the-cias-kryptos-statue/)). The Kryptos group's negative results, roughly 18,000 to 20,000 messages since 2003, sit behind a groups.io login. Its public FAQ says that listing an idea does not mean anyone tested it ([Kryptos FAQ](https://elonka.com/kryptos/faq.html)). The research notes behind this report could not read that archive, so pre-2020 community attempts are undercounted here.

Only two peer-reviewed items attack K4 directly. **Bauer, Link and Molle (*Cryptologia* 2016) tested all 157,248 invertible 2×2 Hill matrices and all roughly 1.63 trillion invertible 3×3 matrices per alphabet numbering**, at one block alignment each. Between 17,000 and 18,000 matrices per alphabet placed BERLIN correctly, but none produced BERLINCLOCK ([T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01611194.2016.1141556)). **Bean (HistoCrypt 2021) reduced the 99,999 base-10, five-digit Gromark primers to 39 that fit all 24 crib letters**, found "none entirely convincing", and argued statistically that K4 is a one-to-one substitution with no transposition ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)). Everything else is informal: Bean's web pages and talks, Schridde's blog, the 2025–2026 repositories, and a flood of self-published "solutions".

The 2025–2026 repositories carry most of the technical weight, and they come with a caveat: **they are single-author, unreviewed, and several were built with Claude or other LLM agents**. They cite one another, but nobody outside the group has audited them ([keithadler](https://github.com/keithadler/kryptos/blob/main/SEARCH.md); [Sovi11](https://github.com/Sovi11/kryptos-k4-cryptanalysis/blob/main/kryptos/findings/SUMMARY.md)). In their favour, keithadler runs 21 planted-cipher controls, Zekios recovers K3 336/336 and plants positive controls, and independent repos reach the same edge cases on their own. For example, keithadler and Zekios both find Quagmire IV satisfiable at period 19 and trace it to the plaintext R to ciphertext P repeat, 38 = 2×19 positions apart ([Zekios](https://github.com/zeyeteam-debug/kryptos-k4-eliminations)). ## Every single-layer family and most two-layer ones are now closed

Exhaustion is uneven, and the useful distinction is between **excluded** (contradicted by the cribs or structure, whatever the key), **exhausted** (a stated space searched to the end), **explored** (tried with no bounds) and **open**. Several exclusions are deductive and firm. **K4 contains two E's (0-based positions 44 and 92) while the cribs need three, so no transposition alone can place them** ([Sovi11](https://github.com/Sovi11/kryptos-k4-cryptanalysis/blob/main/kryptos/findings/SUMMARY.md); [Zekios](https://github.com/zeyeteam-debug/kryptos-k4-eliminations)). The crib conflict graph needs at least three alphabets, which rules out monoalphabetic and homophonic systems. Self-encipherments at positions 33 (S→S) and 74 (K→K) rule out Enigma, Porta, Playfair and anything else without fixed points. All 26 letters appear, which rules out every 25-letter fractionation ([Sovi11](https://github.com/Sovi11/kryptos-k4-cryptanalysis/blob/main/kryptos/findings/SUMMARY.md); [keithadler](https://github.com/keithadler/kryptos/blob/main/SEARCH.md)). Bean showed that seriated digraphic ciphers fail at width 21, where BERLIN would force two plaintext bigrams onto ZT, and at width 7, where NORTHEAST would force two onto BQ ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)).

Two caveats apply across the whole table. First, **closure is relative to the 24 crib letters**. Once a key has more free symbols than the cribs constrain (periods above 26, or two free alphabets at certain periods), "no contradiction" stops meaning "plausible". Those spaces can only be searched by scoring English, and Sovi11 found that the K4 attempts score no better than random text ([Sovi11](https://github.com/Sovi11/kryptos-k4-cryptanalysis/blob/main/kryptos/findings/SUMMARY.md)). Second, some exclusions have been withdrawn. KryptosBot's August 2026 audit found that Bean-style key equalities do not hold across a transposition, which struck its claimed impossibility "for ALL 97! permutations" at most periods ([KryptosBot elimination tiers](https://github.com/jcolinpatrick/kryptos/blob/main/docs/elimination_tiers.md)). Bean-free exact searches still cover the same ground for columnar and rotation, so the conclusions stand. The episode is a reason to prefer exact enumeration over arguments that apply equalities after a transposition.

The sculpture-derived ideas look different from the cipher families. Every physical anomaly has been proposed as a key, but only "HILL" (the extra L on the tableau) has been tested to published bounds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos)). Sanborn has said repeatedly that the misspellings matter for "their orientation or positioning" rather than the letters themselves, yet community attempts have used them as keywords ([Kryptos FAQ](https://elonka.com/kryptos/faq.html)). "Masking" is Scheidt's word. He said he "masked the English language" and called K4 "more than one stage" ([Wired 2005](https://www.wired.com/2005/01/questions-for-kryptos-creator/)). Sanborn was "confused" by the term in 2019 ([rumkin](https://rumkin.com/reference/kryptos/k4/)). The Mengenlehreuhr line ran from Sanborn's 2014 "delve into that particular clock" ([NYT 2014](https://www.nytimes.com/2014/11/21/us/another-kryptos-clue-is-offered-in-a-24-year-old-mystery-at-the-cia.html)) until November 2025, when he said BERLINCLOCK means the World Clock at Alexanderplatz ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cia-kryptos-puzzle-creator-releases-final-clues/)). That statement leaves eleven years of base-5 schedules looking like misdirection.

## The width fingerprints are modest, and 0056 and Sovi11 used different yardsticks

The published statistics on K4 are few. The index of coincidence is **336/(97·96) = 0.0361**, which Sovi11 places at the 21.6th percentile of random 97-letter strings ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109); [Sovi11](https://github.com/Sovi11/kryptos-k4-cryptanalysis/blob/main/kryptos/findings/SUMMARY.md)). Two features stand out. At width 7, five of the six doublets begin in the same column; NSA analysts and Gillogly read this as a sign of substitution plus transposition. At width 21 there are 11 repeated vertical bigrams. Kirchner first reported these in 2003, and Bean put them at about 1 in 6,750 random permutations, though English plaintext written at width 21 averages 9.7 ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)). Bean's crib "minor difference" statistics (1 in 240 to 1 in 35,000) are his main argument for one-to-one substitution. He warns they were found after looking at the data, and they depend on which alphabet numbering is used ([Bean 2024](https://richardbean.id.au/presentations/2024-11-17%20k4-gromark-to-ak.pdf)).

Experiment 0056 reported corrected p ≈ 0.005 for the doublet column and 0.010 for the width-21 repeats (`experiments/0056-width-7-and-21-fingerprints-look-elsewhere/result.json`). Sovi11 reported 0.245 and 0.090–0.419 ([Sovi11](https://github.com/Sovi11/kryptos-k4-cryptanalysis/blob/main/kryptos/findings/SUMMARY.md)). A rerun for this report shows the two are not in conflict: they apply different scan statistics. The rerun used 20,000 letter-preserving shuffles of K4 and widths 2–48, and reproduced 0056's own numbers. 0056 converts each width's count into a tail probability from the shuffles and then takes the smallest probability across widths (the "min-p" scan). Sovi11's figures are matched instead by a raw-threshold scan, which asks whether any width reaches K4's raw count. **Under that rule, "five of six doublets in one residue class" happens at modulus 2 in 19.7% of six-doublet shuffles, and at some modulus in 2–48 in 24.0%, which matches Sovi11's 0.245.** For vertical repeats, "at least 11 at any width" happens in 9.1% of shuffles, which matches the low end of Sovi11's 0.090–0.419. The high end could not be reproduced from the notes. The raw-threshold rule lets the smallest widths dominate, and at those widths five of six is unremarkable, so it overstates how ordinary K4 is. Treating each width on its own scale (min-p) is the standard look-elsewhere correction. On that point 0056 is right.

| Statistic | Nominal p at its width | Min-p scan, 2–48 (0056 method) | Raw-threshold scan, 2–48 | Sovi11 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Doublet column, all shuffles | 0.0005 | 0.0045 (0056: 0.0049) | — | — |
| Doublet column, six-doublet shuffles | 0.0027 | **0.018–0.027** | 0.240 | nominal 0.0022, corrected 0.245 |
| Width-21 repeats, excess count | 0.0003 (≈ Sovi11's 1 in 2,900) | **0.006–0.010** | 0.091 | 0.090–0.419 |
| Width-21 repeats, repeated types | 0.0001 (≈ Bean's 1 in 6,750) | 0.002 | 0.053 | — |

0056's headline doublet figure is still too strong. The unconditional 0.0049 compares K4 against shuffles that often have only three or four doublets. Having six doublets is not unusual in itself (P(≥ 6) = 0.165), so the fair comparison is against six-doublet shuffles. That figure is 0.018 in 0056 (729 shuffles) and 0.027 in the rerun (1,469 shuffles); the gap is Monte Carlo noise. The six-doublet conditional nominal figure (0.0027) also matches Sovi11's nominal 0.0022, which suggests Sovi11 conditioned the same way. Two further corrections apply. First, these two features were picked from a larger set of statistics people have examined (IC, autocorrelation, periodic IC, W spacing, crib distances), and Sovi11's omnibus across seven statistics is p ≈ 0.07–0.13. Second, KryptosBot's audits found the width-21 effect "length-fragile": deleting letters anywhere moves it ([KryptosBot](https://github.com/jcolinpatrick/kryptos)). **The defensible summary is that the doublet column sits around p ≈ 0.02–0.03 and the width-21 repeats around p ≈ 0.002–0.01 after the width correction, and weaker after correcting for the choice of statistic.** That is enough to use them to generate hypotheses, but not as a fitness signal. There is also a tension worth noting. The constructions that naturally produce width-21 structure (a key with period dividing 21, or Gromark with an even base and a five-digit primer) are among those the cribs have already killed ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)). So the width-21 repeats are either chance or the trace of a construction nobody has yet written down.

## 2025–2026 surfaced the plaintext and kept the method private

The 2025–2026 events changed who holds the answer, not what is known about the method. On 2 September 2025 Richard Byrne photographed Sanborn's papers at the Smithsonian Archives of American Art. Jarett Kobek reassembled the 97-letter plaintext from five scrambled strips, which Sanborn says he made in 1990 so the CIA could check the text was not offensive. Sanborn confirmed the text but said "the scrambled plain text was found, but without the coding method or the key" ([AFP via Citizen Digital](https://citizen.digital/article/auction-of-famed-cia-cipher-shaken-after-archive-reveals-code-n373254)). Kobek himself said "there's no way on earth that this is a cryptographic solve" ([Zona Motel](https://zonamotel.substack.com/p/interview-kryptos-k4-uncovered-jarett)). Sanborn's archive, including "the original K4 coding system" and a 1988 alternate K4 plaintext and chart now called K5, sold on 20 November 2025 for **$962,500** ([RR Auction](https://content.rrauction.com/jim-sanborns-complete-kryptos-archive-sells-for-962500-at-auction/)). In June 2026 Paradigm revealed itself as the buyer. It set up a SHA-256/HMAC verifier at $1 a guess, says it chose not to learn the plaintext itself, and reports that AI-assisted attempts have exploded but "none have succeeded yet" ([Paradigm](https://www.paradigm.xyz/writing/kryptos)). One factual dispute remains open: most accounts say Sanborn's files were sealed until 2075, but Sovi11 cites a finding aid revised in May 2026 that says the collection is "open for research" ([Sovi11](https://github.com/Sovi11/kryptos-k4-cryptanalysis/blob/main/kryptos/findings/SUMMARY.md)).

**As of October 2026, no source discloses how K4 was enciphered.** That includes Sanborn, Scheidt, RR Auction, Paradigm, and Kobek and Byrne ([LKN Law](https://lkn-law.com/2026/09/06/kryptos/)). The only new hints are indirect. Sanborn asked "who says it is even a math solution?" ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cia-kryptos-puzzle-creator-releases-final-clues/)). He said K5 is also 97 characters, shares "some coded words in the same positions as K4", and uses a system "similar but not identical" ([AFP](https://citizen.digital/article/auction-of-famed-cia-cipher-shaken-after-archive-reveals-code-n373254)). If identical plaintext words at identical positions give identical ciphertext in K4 and K5, the cipher is keyed by position and not chained to earlier text. That would argue against autokey and against message-specific transposition. The wording is ambiguous, though, and could mean only that the plaintext words share positions. Sanborn's line that his notes "go back to this table" after "double matrixes and triple matrixes" (CNN 2005) is still the most concrete creator account of a multi-stage method ([elonka.com](https://elonka.com/kryptos/)).

The "slightly different tableau" remark, which this database's notes could not trace, has a primary source in the earlier success-criteria research. In the New York Times' 20 November 2010 page "Original Decoding Charts for 'Kryptos'", Sanborn explains in an email how the K1 chart works ("there is a B above the P of palimpsest under that there is an E") and adds: **"I used a tableaux slightly different from the one on 'Kryptos.'"** ([NYT 2010, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/2011id_/http://www.nytimes.com/2010/11/21/us/21codecharts.html)). Two points narrow what this can mean. The remark is about the K1–K2 charts, not K4. And the carved alphabet reproduces his own example: in KRYPTOSABCDEFGHIJLMNQUVWXZ, B (index 8) plus P (index 3) gives E (index 11), and K1 and K2 decrypt in full under the carved KRYPTOS alphabet ([Gillogly 1999](http://www.elonka.com/kryptos/mirrors/cypherpunks/1999/0930.html)). Whatever differed on his working tableau, it did not change K1 or K2, so it was most likely layout (row and header arrangement, or the extra L) rather than letter order. That weakens the reading behind experiment 0071, which tests alphabets one swap or move away from KRYPTOS. The clue register's pointers for C27(c), `research_notes/sanborn_charts/SOURCES.md` and `knowledge/02-sanborn-said.md`, do not contain the quote and should cite the NYT page instead.

## The gaps worth testing are hand conventions, not bigger brute force

For **(a) K1-style tableau substitution combined with a K3-style grid rotation**, the simple family is closed twice over. keithadler tested 30.2 million single and double rotations, widths 2–96, ragged last row, against repeating keys of length 1–26 in all eight tableau families. nitsuah padded to complete grids of 97–108 cells, which includes the 98-cell 7×14 reading with the "?". Both found zero survivors ([keithadler](https://github.com/keithadler/kryptos/blob/main/SEARCH.md); [nitsuah](https://github.com/nitsuah/kryptos/blob/main/docs/analysis/K4_NEGATIVE_SPACE.md)). Zekios added K1's and K2's actual methods (PALIMPSEST and ABSCISSA) chained with K3's rotation in all six orders. The best result was 8 of 24, the same as the shuffled null, while a planted control scored 24 of 24 ([Zekios §19](https://github.com/zeyeteam-debug/kryptos-k4-eliminations)). This project has already covered rotation with ciphertext autokey (0063, 14.2M cases), sampled triple matrices (0037, 200,000 triples), and double rotation up to width 50 against periodic keys (0008). The openings that remain are about how a person working by hand in 1990 would have handled 97 letters. Classical practice offers several conventions: a short row left blank, nulls added to complete the rectangle, blanks at fixed cells, or leftover cells in a grille ([Wikipedia: Transposition cipher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposition_cipher)). The NSA even reconstructed K3 as an "incompletely filled 4×86" keyed columnar ([Black Vault](https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/the-cias-kryptos-statue/)), whereas the coded searches pad only at the ends and only once.

| Rank | Open gap | Why it survives | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| a1 | Rotation with **per-stage padding** (nulls dropped between the two griddings), the short row at the top, or blanks in key-determined cells; with periodic and autokey keys | All coded searches pad once, at the ends | Low: exact crib checks |
| a2 | Rotation + **plaintext autokey** on the KA tableau (primer such as KRYPTOS) | Autokey under transposition is covered only for columnar ≤ 7 publicly; 0063 covered ciphertext autokey only | Low |
| a3 | Rotation sandwiched between **two different tableau keys** (substitute, rotate, substitute) beyond fixed PALIMPSEST/ABSCISSA | Zekios's stacked-key search used columnar 2–7 and width-21 routes, not rotations | Medium |
| a4 | **Exhaustive triple rotation** + tableau | Every public search stops at two stages; 0037 only sampled | Medium |
| a5 | Rotation + periodic key with **two free alphabets at periods 13–26** | Zekios stops at 12; but cribs decide little here, so needs English scoring | High, low power |
| a6 | Rotation + one inserted, deleted or wrong letter | Carving-error sweeps were combined with columnar only | Medium |

For **(b) seven-based constructions**, the direct versions are all closed by exact proofs, several times over: keys of period 7, 14 or 21 with any alphabets, KRYPTOS as a key or autokey primer, width-7 columnar in all 5,040 orders with a periodic key, width-7 seriated digraphic, and Gromark repeating at 21 or 42 ([Zekios](https://github.com/zeyeteam-debug/kryptos-k4-eliminations); [keithadler](https://github.com/keithadler/kryptos/blob/main/SEARCH.md); [Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)). This project has also closed every block-of-seven reshuffle before or after a periodic tableau (0070), the same with ciphertext autokey (0077), and row-keyed grids (0032). Its construction simulations found that only ciphertext autokey at lag 1 with a period-7 key raises the doublet column (1.5% against 0.1–0.3% by chance), and that construction is dead under the cribs (0065, 0066). The clearest remaining opening is **keyed double columnar transposition with one width of 7** (7 × w, or 7 combined with 9, 14 or 15), followed by the tableau. KryptosBot's double-columnar run skipped every pair involving 5 or 7, its register marks width 9 × width 7 "NOT covered", Zekios stopped at 6 × 6, and keithadler lists keyed double columnar as unsearched ([KryptosBot research questions](https://github.com/jcolinpatrick/kryptos/blob/main/docs/research_questions.md)). Next come plaintext autokey under width-7 transposition, and a joint simulation that requires a construction to produce both fingerprints and satisfy the cribs. Since the fingerprints are only modestly unusual once the width choice is corrected for (p ≈ 0.02 and 0.005), these seven-based gaps deserve roughly the same priority as the hand-padding gaps, not more.

## Conclusion

The public record changes what kind of search makes sense. Machine-scale exhaustion has saturated the spaces the cribs can decide, and on the families tested, the 2026 repositories have more computing power than the cipher has free parameters. What survives is a short list of hand procedures nobody has coded: padding handled differently at each stage, blanks at the top or in key-chosen cells, a third grid, an autokey whose primer is a sculpture word, or two different tableau passes around a rotation. Each of these can be checked exactly in minutes, and none of them shows up in the 671 billion configurations already reported. The project's advantage is to test them exhaustively with planted controls and to publish the bounds, so that a negative result enters the shared record and a positive one can be checked against Paradigm's verifier.

Two corrections to the project's own working assumptions follow. The width-7 and width-21 fingerprints are real but weaker than the project has treated them: report the six-doublet conditional figure (about 0.02) rather than 0.005, and use the fingerprints to rank hypotheses, not to score candidates. And the "slightly different tableau" is a documented Sanborn remark about K1–K2 layout that left his ciphertext unchanged, so it argues against spending more effort on near-KRYPTOS alphabets.
