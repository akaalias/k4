# Solvers, analysts and the community

## William H. Webster

Director of Central Intelligence 1987-91, formerly FBI director and a federal judge.
Pushed for art at the agency. At the dedication: "You have captured much of what this
agency is about. We like to be tested. And we enjoy a challenge." Received the sealed
envelope(s), winked, later said on Face the Nation that the Kryptos secret was the
toughest he had to keep, and in 1999: "I have zero memory of this. It was philosophical
and obscure." Sided with Gillogly on computers: "Who set the rules here? This is
precisely what the agencies do." Sanborn: "I wasn't completely truthful with the man."
Died in 2025 at 101. He is the WW of K2.

## The NSA task force (1992)

Bill Studeman, deputy director of the CIA, issued a "gentleman's challenge" ("You guys
are so hot, let's see how hot you are"), relayed by NSA director Mike McConnell at a Gold
Bug award ceremony. Z Group cryptanalysts formed an informal task force in November 1992
and worked on their own time, lunch hours, before and after work, using a PC for the
"grunt work" of frequency distributions but solving by hand. Ken Miller headed the
four-member group and kept a small black notebook of dates; a Wired source says he
deciphered none of the sections himself but wrote up the notes. Dennis McDaniels solved
K3 on his living-room couch in about six hours after volleyball, starting from the single
Q and the five U's; the two others, unnamed, solved K2 and K1. Three parts within two days
of tasking; one day on K4; "a decision was made to stop any further work." Readability
"approximately 89%." Reported up in June 1993; Studeman annotated it "Great Story!"
McDaniels in 2000: "We always believe it will be broken." Miller: "You couldn't be in
this business if you didn't think it was possible." The story surfaced in the Baltimore
Sun in March 2000 and in FOIA documents in 2013-14; the post-2006 analyst summary records
"dozens" at NSA who have tried K4.

## David D. Stein

CIA physicist and senior analyst in the Directorate of Intelligence (an air-defense laser
analyst per the Intellipedia page), not a professional cryptanalyst. Puzzle solver since
childhood. Worked on Kryptos over seven years, about 400 hours, pencil and paper only; no
email account. Split the cipher at the question marks and by per-row letter-frequency
plots into Parts I-V; attacked Part II first because of the DQM repeat; found key length
8 by index of coincidence; was stuck for months; breakthrough on February 21, 1998 by
writing out every shift of each column and scoring columns by log letter-frequency sums,
then cribbing THE (actually THEY) and KNO-W. Walked out to the sculpture to feel the
UNDERGRUUND letter. Solved K3 by modelling the grid as pieces of length B or B-1, taking
END as a hint it was the last column, and ordering columns by digraph frequencies; "the
troublesome Q at the end." Noticed an unexplained I.C. peak at m = 13 in both K1 and K2
and wondered about "another code overlaid on top of the others." On K4: "I haven't spent
much time on this portion, but there are interesting patterns in here as well... I am
confident that it is not impossible." Briefed about 250 people in February 1998 and a
packed auditorium on March 20, 1998; "like a religious experience"; "It's not been solved
and we're finished with it. This is the next phase now." Wrote "Cracking the Courtyard
Crypto" for Studies in Intelligence in 1999, released unredacted by Dunin and via FOIA in
2013 and 2018. "Kryptos was meant to be solved with pencil and paper." Will not look at a
K4 solution he did not find himself.

## Jim Gillogly

Computer scientist, Mentat Inc., past president of the American Cryptogram Association,
had a hand in the FBI's CRYSS software. Looked at Kryptos with pencil in 1992, dropped it,
resumed nine days before June 16, 1999 after an Internet mention, and solved K1-K3 on a
Pentium II with his own classical-cipher program plus "a fair amount of skull sweat,"
from Doug Gwyn's "impressively clean transcription." Phoned the CIA press office, which
told him about Stein. "As far as I'm concerned a crack is where you find it." Described
K3 as "three columnar transpositions." His 1999 guesses for K4: running key, combined
polyalphabetic substitution and transposition, or autokey; "the only likely periodicity
appears to be at period 25, but that may well just be chance"; "a probably-spurious
Phillips-like distribution"; suggested Gromark in 1999 and 2004; flagged Carter's "Yes,
wonderful things" as a possible crib; noted Scheidt's interest in key escrow (1999) and
duress ciphers (2013). "I've tried on the order of 20 billion trial decryptions spread
over two dozen different systems" by 1999, 100 billion by 2005, "shooting in the dark,
since you don't know you've gotten close until you hear a scream." Suspected Scheidt put
the ID BY ROWS alternative in "by a judicious selection of the keyword." Photos on
voynich.net. Per Bean's note, entered the plaintext into Paradigm's verifier laptop in
2026 (Paradigm's own account says Sanborn typed it).

## Doug Gwyn

Posted the first computer transcript to sci.crypt on March 7, 1992 from PHOENIX's
Cryptogram article and an updated one on September 1, 1992 checked against Harry Carter's
video; the standard transcript for eleven years, missing the extra L. Examined the index
of coincidence of the delta text (1999). At the 2005 dinner noted Sanborn's replicas
lacked the L "yet he said that they were supposed to have everything needed to solve."
"My guess is that when it is finally cracked, it will be by somebody versed in classical
C/A who happens to make a lucky guess."

## Elonka Dunin

Game developer and executive producer at Simutronics, St. Louis; now author and
lecturer. Heard of Kryptos in July 2000 while solving the PhreakNIC code; visited the CIA
on October 18, 2002 after a talk on al-Qaeda codes, took rubbings on letter-size paper
and noticed the raised Y, A, R; launched her site on October 24, 2002, which became "a
Kryptos magnet." Co-founded the Yahoo Kryptos group with Gary Warzin in May 2003
(moved to Groups.io in 2019); found the pencil-and-paper K3 method with clean diagonals;
led the Cyrillic Projector translation with her father Stanley and an émigré engineer;
built the Sanborn works catalogue; took the 2003 road trip to both men; filed the NSA
FOIA in March 2010; organizes the Kryptos dinners every couple of years; consulted for
Dan Brown (Nola Kaye in The Lost Symbol is her anagram); co-wrote Codebreaking: A
Practical Guide with Klaus Schmeh (2020, expanded 2023); DEF CON talks on Kryptos (2004,
2024). Her verification standard, written before EAST: "A plausible plaintext which
includes the keywords BERLIN, CLOCK, and NORTHEAST in the proper locations; A method by
which the plaintext has been derived; Sufficient explanation of the method, that an
independent third party can verify the solution." "If they don't have the method, it's
not solved"; "they just get booed out of the room." Someone tells her weekly that they
have solved K4; some callers "have tipped into mental illness." "I just want to see it
solved. I want it off my plate." "Maybe he did something really weird." "He is an artist,
and I think he did something goofy." Believes the solution will be a group effort.

## Klaus Schmeh

German cryptologist, author of Nicht zu knacken and Codeknacker gegen Codemacher, the
Cipherbrain blog (Klausis Krypto Kolumne). Hosted and published the 2015 Kryptos workshop
transcript; posts on each clue with reader attempts in the comments; published the 2020
A.J. Jacobs interview; called Sanborn's progressive difficulty "a clever strategy."

## Richard Bean

School of Information Technology and Electrical Engineering, University of Queensland.
"Kryptos Thoughts" (2018) on the Hill conjecture, keyword alphabets, binary matrices and
the state of the community; HistoCrypt 2020 on hexagram scoring; HistoCrypt 2021
"Cryptodiagnosis of Kryptos K4," the Monte Carlo permutation tests, the Gromark case and
the base-10 length-5 exhaustion with code at github.com/RichardBean/k4testing; The
Conversation (2021) on Callimahos's cryptodiagnosis; talks "From Gromark to Autoclave"
(2024) and "Kryptos Final Guesses" (2025). Thinks "the timeline is absolutely defying the
Scheidt expressed intention," that a mistake in K4 or an unsolvable homebrew is possible,
and that "people spend time on this because of the reputation of Scheidt. Not Sanborn."
Counsels Heuer's Psychology of Intelligence Analysis against wishful thinking, and, in
2018, "not to spend too much time on this and wait for some new information."

## Craig Bauer

Mathematician at York College of Pennsylvania, editor-in-chief of Cryptologia, scholar in
residence at NSA's Center for Cryptologic History; Secret History (2013) and Unsolved!
(2017). With Greg Link and Dante Molle, the Cryptologia 2016 Hill-cipher paper and its
2x2 and 3x3 brute-force searches. Reads HILL as "not subtle at all." Expects "yet another
mystery that the four passages together have a meaning that's greater than their
individual pieces." Within 100 miles of the sculpture and well connected to Sanborn and
Scheidt, which Bean notes.

## Christian Schridde

Numberworld blog. Parts 1-5 on Kryptos (2017-2020): clear mechanics of K1-K3; the 3x3
Hill contradiction and the mod-13 lifts; Wichmann-Hill seeds; Scott's Quagmire-plus-affine
construction and its generalization; the 5-bit ASCII lossiness proof; the DYAHR-as-K3-hint
idea; the suggestion that base 5 belongs to K5 or the riddle; "Kryptos as a coordinate
system" with ABSCISSA as the x-axis; the view that Sanborn "probably did something
completely different from the first three ciphers, something more visual."
