# K4's plaintext is English; the method is the prize

The full evidence supports half of the core assumption and breaks the other half. Everyone who has seen the K4 plaintext — Sanborn, the two journalists who assembled it from scraps in his Smithsonian papers in September 2025, and the auction house that sold his archive — describes **a fixed 97-letter English passage** that "makes semantic sense," carries BERLIN CLOCK and EAST NORTHEAST at the published offsets, and is checked by exact string match: Sanborn's hidden copy in 2005, a friend's "what is at letter-position 27?" in 2010, and since June 2026 **Paradigm's SHA-256/HMAC verifier at $1 per attempt**, which Paradigm itself cannot read ([Paradigm](https://www.paradigm.xyz/writing/kryptos); [NYT via Wayback](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/10/16/science/kryptos-cia-solution-sanborn-auction.html)). So "readable English you either have or don't" is literally how the only remaining gatekeeper works. But the same evidence shows that having the words has never been the success standard and is now explicitly rejected as one: Sanborn confirmed the archive text was accurate and in the same breath said "K4 has not been solved… it has been discovered," because "the scrambled plain text was found, but without the coding method or the key" ([RR Auction](https://content.rrauction.com/kryptos-k4-discovered-not-solved-heres-what-actually-happened/); [AFP](https://phys.org/news/2025-11-auction-famed-cia-cipher-shaken.html)). Three further facts should shape any automated loop. First, the plaintext is itself a riddle that "is not going to be finished when it's decoded" and now points to a sibling 97-character ciphertext, K5, whose ciphertext Paradigm holds and plans to publish ([NPR 2020](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/801323608); [Paradigm](https://www.paradigm.xyz/writing/kryptos)). Second, Sanborn's three solved sections each contain exactly one deliberate misspelling, he calls his work "rife with mistakes on purpose," and Scheidt says he "masked the English language" in K4, so a language-model scorer tuned to clean English can reject the right answer ([Wired 2005](https://www.wired.com/2005/01/questions-for-kryptos-creator/); [kryptools](https://www.kryptools.com.about.ag/hints.htm)). Third, the K2 precedent proves that a fluent, statistically perfect English decrypt can still be wrong in a way only the author can detect: NSA, CIA and Gillogly all accepted "ID BY ROWS" for 7 to 14 years before Sanborn revealed the ending was "X LAYER TWO" ([Kryptos Group 2006](https://elonka.com/kryptos/CorrectedK2Announcement.html)). The practical conclusion is that a loop should treat the Paradigm oracle as the final arbiter of the string, use the 24 crib letters and letter-count constraints as hard filters, score plaintext candidates with tolerance for orthographic noise, and define its own success as "a reproducible method that regenerates all 97 letters," because that, not the string, is what the community, the artist, and the buyer will credit.

## K1 to K3 set the precedent: fluent text plus a replicable method, with the author's silence taken as assent

The three solved sections establish what "solved" has meant in practice and what it has failed to catch. K1 is **63 letters of Sanborn's own prose**, enciphered as a Quagmire-III keyed Vigenère (both alphabets keyed on KRYPTOS, index letter K) under the ten-letter key PALIMPSEST, reading "Between subtle shading and the absence of light lies the nuance of iqlusion" ([Gillogly 1999](https://elonka.com/kryptos/mirrors/cypherpunks/1999/0930.html); [NSA FOIA via Black Vault](https://documents.theblackvault.com/documents/controversies/KRYPTOS-Statue-NSA.pdf)). K2 is **369 letters under the eight-letter key ABSCISSA**, a telegraphic report with X separators and three literal question marks that pass through unenciphered, geographic coordinates, and the initials WW, which Sanborn confirmed in 2005 mean William Webster ([Kryptos Group 2006](https://elonka.com/kryptos/CorrectedK2Announcement.html); [Wired 2005](https://www.wired.com/2005/01/questions-for-kryptos-creator/)). K3 is **336 letters of transposition**, a tightened paraphrase of Howard Carter's 1923 account of opening Tutankhamun's antechamber that ends on Carnarvon's unanswered question "Can you see anything?" bracketed by an X and a Q; Carter's reply, "Yes, wonderful things," is not on the sculpture, and Gillogly flagged it in 1999 as a possible K4 crib ([Carter 1923](https://archive.org/download/tomboftutankhame00cart_1/tomboftutankhame00cart_1_djvu.txt); [Gillogly 1999](https://elonka.com/kryptos/mirrors/cypherpunks/1999/0930.html)).

Each section carries exactly one misspelling — IQLUSION, UNDERGRUUND, DESPARATLY — and the mechanics of those errors matter for how a K4 verifier should treat orthography. Sanborn's coding chart, published by the New York Times in 2010, shows the K1 keyword written PALIMPCEST over a correctly spelled ILLUSION; enciphering ILLUSION under PALIMPCEST and IQLUSION under PALIMPSEST produce the identical ciphertext that is on the copper, so the celebrated misspelling may be an adopted artefact of a keyword slip ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos); [NYT charts 2010](https://web.archive.org/web/2011id_/http://www.nytimes.com/2010/11/21/us/21codecharts.html)). UNDERGRUUND arises because the chart spells UNDERGROUND correctly with ciphertext E, while the sculpture carries R at that position; the copper is not a faithful copy of the charts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos)). Sanborn's public line is that the errors are deliberate and that "it wasn't what they were that was so important, as their orientation or positioning," a remark nobody has decoded ([Elonka FAQ](https://elonka.com/kryptos/faq.html)). The NSA's own write-up noted DESPARATLY "should be DESPERATELY, but it did indeed decrypt as shown" and treated the three misspellings as possible K4 clues ([NSA summary](https://web.archive.org/web/20220514060258id_/https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/cia-kryptos-sculpture/KRYPTOS_Summary.pdf)).

Three teams solved K1 to K3 independently, and none needed Sanborn. An NSA Z Group task force, challenged by CIA deputy director Studeman, solved all three **within two days of informal tasking in November 1992**, spent one more day on K4, and stopped because "the last section is too short to solve without diverting a great deal of effort from operational problems"; the formal memo went to DIRNSA in June 1993 ([Wired 2013](https://www.wired.com/2013/07/nsa-cracked-kryptos-before-cia/); [NSA FOIA via Black Vault](https://documents.theblackvault.com/documents/controversies/KRYPTOS-Statue-NSA.pdf)). CIA physicist David Stein solved them with **pencil and paper over roughly 400 hours**, breaking through on February 21, 1998, and presented to a packed Langley auditorium; he split the panel at its question marks, found key length 8 for K2 by index of coincidence, and treated the leading "END" of K3 as a hint that it was the final column ([Stein 1999](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB431/docs/intell_ebb_010.PDF)). Jim Gillogly solved them by computer in June 1999 after nine days, working "from an impressively clean transcription by Doug Gwyn," and his cypherpunks post made the result publicly checkable; the community credits him as first solver precisely because he published in a way third parties could verify ([Gillogly 1999](https://elonka.com/kryptos/mirrors/cypherpunks/1999/0930.html); [Elonka FAQ](https://elonka.com/kryptos/faq.html)).

What "solved" meant in each case was a unique, fluent English text derived by a stated method and replicated independently; author confirmation was passive. The NSA recorded its unresolved items honestly ("The meaning of 'I.D. BY ROWS' is not known at this time"), Scheidt told Stein "the solution is correct, but you didn't do it the way I intended you to do it," and K3 in particular has been "solved" by at least five mechanically different routes — NSA's keyed 4×86 columnar, Stein's column pieces, Gillogly's "three columnar transpositions," Stehle's every-192nd-letter formula, Dunin's diagonals — with the NSA cautioning that its route "may or may not be the way the system was originally set up by Sanborn" ([WashPost 1999](https://web.archive.org/web/20160616083312/http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/national/daily/july99/kryptos19.htm); [NSA FOIA via Black Vault](https://documents.theblackvault.com/documents/controversies/KRYPTOS-Statue-NSA.pdf); [Elonka part 3](https://elonka.com/kryptos/part3.html)). That is the first lesson for a loop: for K3 the plaintext was unique and verifiable but the encipherment route was underdetermined, so "recover the text" and "recover the author's algorithm" were already separable goals in 1999.

The 2006 correction is the decisive precedent against trusting readability. On April 19, 2006 Sanborn called Elonka Dunin to say "No, that last part is wrong": he had dropped one ciphertext letter from the end of a K2 line for aesthetic balance, expected the tail to decrypt as "an unintelligible scramble," and was "astounded" that by chance it read ID BY ROWS; inserting a ciphertext S eight letters from the end re-phases ABSCISSA and yields **WEST X LAYER TWO** ([Kryptos Group 2006](https://elonka.com/kryptos/CorrectedK2Announcement.html); [Wired 2006](https://www.wired.com/2006/04/typo-confounds-kryptos-sleuths/)). When Stein read him "ID by rows" in 1998 Sanborn "thought Stein was saying some crypto jargon about decryption technique," which shows that he heard the solutions but did not audit them letter by letter until he compared plaintext and copper for a planned book ([Wired 2006](https://www.wired.com/2006/04/typo-confounds-kryptos-sleuths/)). Kryptos Group member Monet Friedrich had found PLAYERTWO six months earlier by rotating the key to BSCISSAA, so the community had the right answer in hand and could not tell it from the wrong one ([Elonka timeline](https://elonka.com/kryptos/KryptosTimeline.html)). The community's post-2010 criteria — plausible plaintext with cribs in place, a method, and enough explanation for independent reproduction — are the formalisation of this episode ([Elonka FAQ](https://elonka.com/kryptos/faq.html)).

Across the three texts Sanborn's authorial signature is consistent and predictive. He uses X as an enciphered full stop and literal "?" glyphs as unenciphered punctuation, asks a rising number of direct questions, and returns to a vocabulary of invisibility, burial, location and layers: "absence of light," "totally invisible," "earth's magnetic field," "buried out there somewhere," coordinates, a sealed tomb, and keys that mean overwritten text (PALIMPSEST) and a coordinate (ABSCISSA) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos); [Stein 1999](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB431/docs/intell_ebb_010.PDF)). He has said outright that "I have left instructions in the earlier text that refer to later text" and that clues to K4 are contained in the solved parts, though in 2019 he also told Klaus Schmeh it is not necessary to know K1 to K3 to solve K4 ([Wired 2005](https://www.wired.com/2005/01/questions-for-kryptos-creator/); [Schmeh 2019](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2019/03/15/cnn-documentary-about-kryptos-a-making-of-report-with-many-photographs/)). The one-misspelling-per-section pattern predicts one in K4; the literal-punctuation pattern explains why the "?" before OBKR is contested territory.

## K4's 97 letters, four cribs and the structure they impose

The K4 ciphertext is **97 letters, OBKR…CAR**, identical across Wikipedia, Dunin's transcript, rumkin, Bean's HistoCrypt paper and Schmeh's blog: `OBKRUOXOGHULBSOLIFBBWFLRVQQPRNGKSSOTWTQSJQSSEKZZWATJKLUDIAWINFBNYPVTTMZFPKWGDKZXTJCDIGKUHUAUEKCAR` ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos); [Elonka transcript](https://elonka.com/kryptos/transcript.html)). On the copper, OBKR sits at the end of the last K3 line immediately after the fourth "?", followed by three full 31-character lines ([Elonka transcript](https://elonka.com/kryptos/transcript.html)). The "97 versus 98" argument is entirely about that question mark: Sanborn has said K4 is "exactly 97 characters long" and that BERLIN "starts at exactly the 64th character," and all four published crib positions use O-of-OBKR as position 1, so the de facto convention is settled even though Bean notes a 98-character variant would make "seven pairs of rows with seven characters per row" ([rumkin K4](https://rumkin.com/reference/kryptos/k4/); [Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)). The 97th character is the final R of CAR; no source gives it special status beyond the fact that 97 is prime, which Bean uses as an argument against block ciphers ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)). Scheidt said in 2005 "I think there are 98 characters left," which is the likeliest origin of the press's 98 ([scirealm hints](https://scirealm.org/KryptosHints.html)).

The four cribs, in release order, are BERLIN at **positions 64–69 (ciphertext NYPVTT)**, provided to the New York Times on November 20, 2010 for the sculpture's twentieth anniversary; CLOCK at **70–74 (MZFPK)**, given to the Times on November 20, 2014 for the twenty-fifth anniversary of the Wall's fall; NORTHEAST at **26–34 (QQPRNGKSS)**, in a Times interactive of January 29, 2020 billed as the "final clue"; and EAST at **22–25 (FLRV)**, released privately to several solvers in April 2020 and made public by Times reporter John Schwartz's tweet of August 24, 2020 ([NYT 2010](https://web.archive.org/web/20230419162546id_/https://www.nytimes.com/2010/11/21/us/21code.html?hp); [NYT 2014](https://web.archive.org/web/20240224133656id_/https://www.nytimes.com/2014/11/21/us/another-kryptos-clue-is-offered-in-a-24-year-old-mystery-at-the-cia.html); [NYT 2020](https://web.archive.org/web/20240504215836/https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2020/01/29/climate/kryptos-sculpture-final-clue.html); [Schmeh Aug 2020](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2020/08/24/jim-sanborn-publishes-another-new-kryptos-clue/)). Together they fix **24 of 97 plaintext letters**: FLRVQQPRNGKSS = EASTNORTHEAST and NYPVTTMZFPK = BERLINCLOCK ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)). In November 2025 Sanborn added four thematic hints that specify no positions and so are not cribs: two events figure in the solution, his second trip to Egypt in late 1986 and the fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989; "BERLINCLOCK" refers to the **World Clock (Weltzeituhr) in Berlin**, "the gathering place for the crowds that brought down the Berlin wall," not the base-5 Mengenlehreuhr he had steered solvers toward in 2014; the codes are about "delivering a message"; and K5 is thematically tied to K2's "its buried out there somewhere" ([Scientific American Nov 2025](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cia-kryptos-puzzle-creator-releases-final-clues/); [Popular Mechanics via AOL](https://www.aol.com/lifestyle/cia-home-unsolved-puzzle-35-130000201.html)). The 2014 steer ("You'd better delve into that particular clock," referring to Dieter Binninger's set-theory clock) absorbed roughly eleven years of community effort on base-5 key schedules before the 2025 correction ([NYT 2014](https://web.archive.org/web/20240224133656id_/https://www.nytimes.com/2014/11/21/us/another-kryptos-clue-is-offered-in-a-24-year-old-mystery-at-the-cia.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos)).

The statistics of the 97 letters are flat and the cribs are many-to-many. The index of coincidence is **0.036**, essentially random (English is about 0.067), all 26 letters occur, and K appears eight times; this rules out pure transposition and monoalphabetic substitution at once ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109); [NSA summary](https://web.archive.org/web/20220514060258id_/https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/cia-kryptos-sculpture/KRYPTOS_Summary.pdf)). Within the cribs the same ciphertext letter decrypts to different plaintext letters (Q→N and O at adjacent positions 26–27; S→S and T at 33–34; T→I and N at 68–69; F→E and O; V→T and L), the same plaintext letter encrypts to up to three ciphertext letters (E→F, G, Y; T→R, S, V), P→R occurs at both 28 and 66, and **two letters encrypt to themselves** (S at 33, K at 74), which excludes Enigma-class reflector machines and standard Playfair ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos)). Two older observations survive: at width 7 five of the six horizontal doublets (BB, QQ, SS, ZZ, TT) fall in the same column, and at width 21 the 76 vertical bigrams contain 11 repeats, a pattern seen in about 1 in 6,750 random permutations; Bean shows the cribs kill the seriated-digraphic explanation of both widths ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109); [Numberworld 2017](https://www.numberworld.blog/2017/03/kryptos-cipher-part-2.html)). Bean's most distinctive finding is that crib plaintext letters belonging to KRYPTOS sit unusually close to their ciphertext letters in the alphabet (ten distances summing to 21, about 1 in 5,520 permutations), which he reads as "one-to-one encryption of single letters to single letters… no transposition involved," and which motivates his Gromark hypothesis ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)). Sanborn has reportedly confirmed in private that NYPVTT→BERLIN is "a one-to-one match with plain B taken, has the encipherment done to it, and out pops a cipher N," though he "backed off" when pressed, and the 2010 Times article's phrase that "the other 91 characters and their proper order are yet to be determined" keeps a reordering step on the table ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109); [kryptools](https://www.kryptools.com.about.ag/hints.htm); [NYT 2010](https://web.archive.org/web/20230419162546id_/https://www.nytimes.com/2010/11/21/us/21code.html?hp)).

Two physical anomalies are routinely connected to K4. The letters Y, A, R in the first line of the lower-left plate (ENDYAHROHN…) are raised about a centimetre, the only superscript letters on the sculpture; the NSA wondered whether END marked the end of a portion and whether "the Y, A and R floating in the air a bit higher… may signify something else" ([Elonka main page](https://elonka.com/kryptos/); [NSA summary](https://web.archive.org/web/20220514060258id_/https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/cia-kryptos-sculpture/KRYPTOS_Summary.pdf)). On the tableau side, row N carries an extra trailing L so that the rightmost column reads HILL down rows L to O; Bauer, Link and Molle proposed a Hill cipher on that basis, and the only artist-side signal that the L is deliberate is that Sanborn left it out of the small models he sold ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos); [Bean 2018](https://kryptos.richardbean.id.au/kryptos-2018.htm)). Both anomalies sit on the top line of the two lower plates, a positional coincidence that is observation, not documented design.

## Sanborn and Scheidt describe a masked English phrase that is itself a riddle

Both designers say K4 is English and both say that reading it is not the end. Sanborn to NPR in January 2020: "It's a 97-character phrase. And that phrase is in itself a riddle. It's mysterious. It's going to lead to something else. **It's not going to be finished when it's decoded**" ([NPR 2020](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/801323608)). That is the same position he took before the sculpture was finished — "They will be able to read what I wrote, but what I wrote is a mystery itself… What I wrote out were clues to a larger mystery" in a 1991 AP-derived piece, "a riddle within a riddle — one that will be solvable only after the four encrypted passages are known" to the Times in 1999, and "the final passage will lead in several directions. The word directions is — I use intentionally" to NPR in 2010 ([Cryptogram 1991](https://www.thekryptosproject.com/kryptos/cia/thecryptogram/pdfs/Binder2.pdf); [NYT 1999](https://web.archive.org/web/20231020061426/https://archive.nytimes.com/www.nytimes.com/library/tech/99/06/biztech/articles/16code.html); [NPR 2010](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/131520768)). In 2005 he said the answer "will be far more ambiguous" than K1 to K3, that the text refers to "an act that took place when I was at the agency and a location that's on the grounds of the agency," that you do not have to be on CIA grounds to solve it but "you have to decipher the piece and then go to the agency and find that place," and, asked whether he buried anything, "Oh, I won't say" ([Wired 2005](https://www.wired.com/2005/01/questions-for-kryptos-creator/)). What changed in 2025 is that the "something else" acquired a name and a form: K5 ([Open letter Aug 2025](https://www.elonka.com/kryptos/OpenLetterAug2025.html)).

On method Sanborn is consistent about three things: he is not a mathematician, he modified what Scheidt gave him, and he will not say how. He told Wired in 2005 that Scheidt "gave me a whole variety of possible systems to use and ways to modify all of those systems," that "I did use some matrix codes Ed gave me, and I have also designed visual systems for encoding, which are much harder for cryptographers to crack because they're individualistic," that he used "five or six" techniques in all, and that Scheidt "doesn't know the solution… I'd be modifying systems and developing my own, which would make it virtually impossible for him to decipher all of it" ([Wired 2005](https://www.wired.com/2005/01/questions-for-kryptos-creator/)). To NPR in 2020: "I am lousy at math to the point where I consider myself to be anathamath… I can employ methods that don't have anything to do with mathematics" ([NPR 2020](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/801323608)). To the Financial Times in 2025, of Scheidt's system: "**It is a solid system, and I fucked with it.** 'What do you mean you fucked with it?' 'I can't say'" ([FT Weekend 2025](https://freight.cargo.site/m/S2500073053613302968094958969384/kryptos.pdf)). At the Spy Museum in November 2025, deflecting a question about the mathematics: "Who says it is even a math solution?" and "I was fortunate not to understand mathematics, probably in my ability to make the code" ([Scientific American Nov 2025](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cia-kryptos-puzzle-creator-releases-final-clues/)). In 2009 he described "a coding system that would unravel itself slowly over a period of time," and in 2005 he told the Wall Street Journal "The most obvious key to the sculpture, nobody has picked up on" ([kryptools](https://www.kryptools.com.about.ag/hints.htm); [WSJ via Post-Gazette 2005](https://web.archive.org/web/20070217114750id_/http://www.post-gazette.com/pg/05147/511693.stm)).

Scheidt's account is the only technical description from either designer, and "masking" is its centre. In January 2005: "The first three processes were designed so that a person could, through cryptographic analysis, have access to the English. And the last process, **I masked the English language** so it's more of a challenge now"; "The masking technique may not be known"; "you need to solve the technique first and then go for the puzzle"; and, on whether a readable result ends things, "Once it is readable, it may have other encoding that's involved in it. We may establish a code that 1 o'clock on Friday is equal to 'cake'" ([kryptools](https://www.kryptools.com.about.ag/hints.htm); [scirealm hints](https://scirealm.org/KryptosHints.html)). To NPR in June 2005 he explained E-versus-Z frequency analysis and said that in part four he deliberately masked that advantage; the reporter's gloss that sleuths speculate this "could mean anything from random Q's and X's being thrown in to phonetic spellings" is not Scheidt's ([NPR 2005 mirror](https://www.elonka.com/kryptos/mirrors/npr/2005transcript.html)). In 2009: "There may be more to the puzzle than what you see. Just because you broke it doesn't mean you have the answer," and "The intelligence picture includes mirrors and obfuscation" ([Wired 2009 via metzdowd](https://www.metzdowd.com/pipermail/cryptography/2009-April/015266.html)). At a 2011 Kryptos dinner he said K4 "is not mathematical (although this does not preclude it being modeled mathematically), it is simple, can be remembered, and executed years later when used with the correct key word/s," and is "similar to what would be provided agents or pilots in case of capture" ([Bean 2021 citing Hannon 2011](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)). At the 2015 workshop: "no, I would consider it more than one stage"; "there's a masking technique that applied so that the English language wouldn't be available"; "the K4 may not be something as such" (unlike the other sections, which "have all the intrinsics of classical cryptographic puzzles"); "I only know what I did"; and, asked whether he would have known if Sanborn made an encoding error, "no" ([Workshop transcript 2015](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/kryptos-workshop-transcript/)). In 2020 he added "if you can change the language base then it becomes in my favor… when it was used as the mask it was current," and in 2022 that he had wanted an extinct language but "They said no" ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109); [Northern Virginia Magazine 2022](https://northernvirginiamag.com/culture/2022/10/14/cia-kryptos-sculpture-cipher/)). The most literal reading, consistent with all of this, is a short memorable keyword system applied to a plaintext that has first been transformed so its letter statistics are no longer English; nothing he says implies the plaintext is non-English, numeric, or unreadable.

"Layer two" and "masking" are the two points where the designers' statements fail to meet. Sanborn confirmed X LAYER TWO as the correct K2 ending in 2006 "but refused to reveal anything more"; the folding and second-cipher-layer readings are community inference, and in 2025 he tied K5, not K4, to the K2 text ([Kryptos Group 2006](https://elonka.com/kryptos/CorrectedK2Announcement.html); [Scientific American Nov 2025](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cia-kryptos-puzzle-creator-releases-final-clues/)). When Dunin raised Scheidt's "masking technique" with Sanborn at a March 2019 lunch, "Jim was confused when we mentioned masking technique, evidently it's something that Ed said that Jim didn't understand," and he would confirm only the 97-character length and the BERLIN position ([rumkin K4](https://rumkin.com/reference/kryptos/k4/)). The record contains further contradictions a loop designer should hold in mind: Sanborn said in 1999 that the complete answer was handed to Webster and in 2005 that "I definitely didn't give him the last section"; Scheidt expected the whole puzzle solved in "roughly five years" or under seven while Sanborn told ABC in 1991 it might take "weeks, months, years, or never"; Sanborn in 2005 said "The secret will probably pass away," then that it was in his will, in 2009 "I'd rather die knowing it wasn't cracked," in 2020 a posthumous auction, and in 2025 a lifetime sale; Kobek alleges Sanborn said at a 2019 dinner that he "did not care" what method was used, against his 2025 insistence that method and key are "a very important distinction" ([Wired 2005](https://www.wired.com/2005/01/questions-for-kryptos-creator/); [Baltimore Sun 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/20140209165252/http://articles.baltimoresun.com/2000-03-17/news/0003180448_1_decipher-petrified-wood-cia-headquarters); [Wired 2009 via metzdowd](https://www.metzdowd.com/pipermail/cryptography/2009-April/015266.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos)). Scheidt's own verdicts on K4 have also drifted from "I know what the message was to be" and "I saved the best for last" (1999–2005) to "To be honest, I don't know the answer. After Jim finished the sculpture, I never went back to check the code" (2015), which is reconcilable only if he designed the method and knew the intended text but never saw Sanborn's final ciphertext ([WashPost 1999](https://web.archive.org/web/20160616083312/http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/national/daily/july99/kryptos19.htm); [Intel Today 2019](https://inteltoday.org/2019/11/09/kryptos-week-2019-section-iv-a-few-clues-from-edward-scheidt/)).

## In 2025 and 2026 the plaintext leaked, sold for $962,500, and became a $1 hash check

Sanborn's open letter of August 14, 2025, hosted by Dunin and quoted by the Washington Post, announced that "the plain text — the secret of K4" would be sold at auction on his 80th birthday, November 20, 2025, because after "literally tens of thousands of emails and letters" he no longer had "the physical, mental, or financial resources" to maintain the section; AI had become "a recent curse… responsible for a flood of meaningless decrypts"; he was building an AI-based "verification process" transferable to the new owner; he hoped the owner would keep the secret because "Power resides with a secret, not without it"; and "even when K4 has been solved, its riddle will persist as K5" ([Open letter Aug 2025](https://www.elonka.com/kryptos/OpenLetterAug2025.html); [WaPo via The Nightly](https://thenightly.com.au/culture/the-kryptos-code-at-the-cia-headquarters-has-gone-unsolved-for-35-years-now-its-up-for-sale-c-19690600)). The Post added that he had instituted a $50 fee for guesses, which the Times reported earns about $40,000 a year, and that he had faced death threats and installed panic buttons ([WaPo via The Nightly](https://thenightly.com.au/culture/the-kryptos-code-at-the-cia-headquarters-has-gone-unsolved-for-35-years-now-its-up-for-sale-c-19690600); [Artnet](https://news.artnet.com/art-world/cia-kryptos-sculpture-code-auction-2677451)).

Before the sale closed, the plaintext surfaced by library science rather than cryptanalysis. On September 2, 2025 Richard Byrne photographed Sanborn's publicly accessible papers at the Smithsonian's Archives of American Art at Jarett Kobek's request; among the images Kobek found five pages of handwritten sentences cut into strips and taped together out of order, saw "BERLIN CLOCK," recognised that the remainder matched the expected character count and "makes semantic sense," and assembled **97 characters with the known cribs "exactly in the right position"** ([Zona Motel interview](https://zonamotel.substack.com/p/interview-kryptos-k4-uncovered-jarett); [NYT via Wayback](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/10/16/science/kryptos-cia-solution-sanborn-auction.html)). They emailed Sanborn on September 3 with the first words of the passage as the subject line; he confirmed it in a half-hour call, explained that the scraps were 1990 copies made to show the CIA's Department of Historical Intelligence the text was not offensive and had been filed by mistake while he gathered papers during treatment for metastatic cancer about a decade earlier, proposed NDAs plus a share of auction proceeds, which they refused as potentially making them "party to fraud," and had the Smithsonian seal the files until 2075; RR Auction's lawyers threatened copyright and tortious-interference claims if they published ([NYT via Wayback](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/10/16/science/kryptos-cia-solution-sanborn-auction.html); [Scientific American Oct 2025](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/a-solution-to-the-cias-kryptos-code-is-found-after-35-years/)). Kobek's own framing was unambiguous: "Rich and I recovered the plaintext. There's no way on earth that this is a cryptographic solve," and his broader argument was that "this is not necessarily something that's ever going to be solved by cryptanalysis or STEM or math" ([Zona Motel interview](https://zonamotel.substack.com/p/interview-kryptos-k4-uncovered-jarett)). Sanborn's response set the current standard: "The headlines say they found the 'solution' or the 'key.' These are both very incorrect terms… They did not solve K4 and they certainly did not find the key"; "K4 has not been solved. K4 has been discovered and it points in the direction of K5" ([RR Auction blog](https://content.rrauction.com/kryptos-k4-discovered-not-solved-heres-what-actually-happened/)). Dunin, the community's gatekeeper since 2003, said "One thing is to have the words. It's another to have the method… if they can't show the method, they just get booed out of the room," and Scientific American stated the rule flatly: "To claim a solution, one must show how they decoded it from that ciphertext" ([Scientific American Oct 2025](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/a-solution-to-the-cias-kryptos-code-is-found-after-35-years/)).

The lot that sold on November 20, 2025 was Sanborn's entire private archive, not just the words. RR Auction's lot 2001 listed seventeen items, including the original handwritten K4 plaintext with a signed letter from Ed Scheidt, "the original coding system for K4," the K1–K3 coding charts, an unpublished 1988 alternate K1 plaintext and chart, "original unpublished 1988 alternate K4 plaintext and coding chart now known as K5," the scrambled texts shown to the CIA, a 1988 copper proof plate, and a private afternoon in which Sanborn would walk the buyer through everything including K5; items 1–7 were not photographed "for reasons of secrecy," RR disclaimed having examined them, and bidders acknowledged the plaintext "may become publicly known through independent research or disclosure" ([RR Auction lot](https://www.rrauction.com/auctions/lot-detail/350761607302001-the-complete-secrets-of-kryptos-jim-sanborns-private-archive/)). It realised **$962,500 including premium against a $300,000–$500,000 estimate**, in a sale totalling $1,947,111, to an anonymous buyer "preparing a long-term stewardship plan" ([RR Auction press release](https://content.rrauction.com/jim-sanborns-complete-kryptos-archive-sells-for-962500-at-auction/)). Sanborn, 79 and with "a series of health scares," told the AP "nobody has the method but me" and that the correspondence "was miserable, and it's still miserable" ([AP via MyNorthwest](https://mynorthwest.com/national/solution-goes-on-auction-for-cia-hqs-kryptos-sculpture-that-has-stumped-code-breakers-for-decades/4156053)).

On June 12, 2026 the venture firm Paradigm, in a post signed by Dan Robinson and Matt Huang, disclosed that it was the buyer and "the new stewards of the Kryptos secret," and that it had chosen "not to ruin the mystery even for ourselves." The verification design is the detail that matters most for any solver: "We set up a new computer with a terminal for Jim to enter the plaintext solution to K4. On that device, a program ran the plaintext through a one-way function (a SHA256 hash). It sent the hash to Google's Cloud Key Management Service, which generated a unique verification tag (a hash-based message authentication code, or HMAC)… The plaintext never left the device, and we wiped the laptop afterward." Submissions at paradigm.xyz/kryptos/k4 cost **$1 via Stripe "to prevent brute forcing,"** the prize for K4 is "The answer," and Paradigm also received "the ciphertext for another 97-character puzzle, known as K5, that he created at the same time," which it "plan[s] to release in the future" ([Paradigm](https://www.paradigm.xyz/writing/kryptos)). The accompanying ten-puzzle capture-the-flag (PK1–PK10, $1,000 each) is Paradigm's own material, created because "there has been an explosion in AI-assisted attempts on Kryptos, but none have succeeded yet. One possible reason is that there is no feedback loop that solvers can use to test if their tools are getting better"; K4 carries no prize ([Paradigm CTF rules](https://paradigm.xyz/kryptos-ctf/rules)). As of October 14, 2026 the Paradigm K4 page still reads "Unsolved," leaderboards show PK1 and PK4 solved within hours on June 12, PK8 on September 6, and PK9 and PK10 on October 2, a community project log of October 11 records "no authenticated solution," Dunin's site has not been updated past the August 2025 announcement, no lawsuit has been filed, and Scheidt has said nothing publicly about any of it ([Paradigm K4](https://www.paradigm.xyz/kryptos/k4); [Paradigm PK10](https://paradigm.xyz/kryptos-ctf/pk10); [markmnl/kryptos](https://github.com/markmnl/kryptos); [Elonka main page](https://www.elonka.com/kryptos/)).

Three consequences follow. The verifier tests plaintext equality only, so a correct-words submission from any source would pass, which is why words alone cannot satisfy the Sanborn–Dunin standard. The comparison is an HMAC of whatever Sanborn typed, so the submission must presumably match his string exactly — case, spacing, and any deliberate misspelling included — and no normalisation rules are published; a loop should test canonicalisation conventions before spending queries, and should expect no partial feedback ([Paradigm](https://www.paradigm.xyz/writing/kryptos)). And the plaintext now exists in writing in at least three places outside Sanborn's head — Paradigm's unread archive, the sealed Smithsonian scraps, and Kobek and Byrne's assembly — none of which is a usable verification path, so Paradigm's oracle is the only one ([RR Auction lot](https://www.rrauction.com/auctions/lot-detail/350761607302001-the-complete-secrets-of-kryptos-jim-sanborns-private-archive/); [Scientific American Oct 2025](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/a-solution-to-the-cias-kryptos-code-is-found-after-35-years/)).

## Thirty-five years of attacks have exhausted the classical families but not the masked one

Only two peer-reviewed papers attack K4 directly, and they contradict each other. Bauer, Link and Molle (Cryptologia, 2016) examined "evidence that the fourth makes use of matrix encryption" from the tableau's HILL column and reported brute-force attacks on the 2×2 and 3×3 Hill cases, with "room for further testing" — that is, no solution; Bean's critique is that they tested only one of five block alignments and ignored Keith Edkins's February 2004 exhaustive search of all 157,248 invertible 2×2 matrices ([Bauer et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/01611194.2016.1141556); [Bean 2018](https://kryptos.richardbean.id.au/kryptos-2018.htm)). Bean's own HistoCrypt 2021 "Cryptodiagnosis" argues from the crib statistics for a strictly letter-to-letter cipher with no transposition, proposes the ACA Gromark family (a numeric running key expanded from a short primer, possibly with a KRYPTOS-keyed alphabet), and reports the only community search that can be called exhausted with explicit bounds: the 24 cribs yield 21 inequalities and one equality on key digits (k27 = k65 from R→P at positions 28 and 66), reducing **99,999 base-10 length-5 primers to 1,040 and then, via Gröbner bases, to 39**, each tested with simulated-annealing alphabet search; "although many plaintexts close to ordinary English were discovered, none were entirely convincing" ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109); [k4testing code](https://github.com/RichardBean/k4testing)). The NSA's declassified post-2006 summary records the agency's own dead ends: a "fairly flat distribution, although there are eight Ks," "a slight interval 7 property," plaintext- and ciphertext-autokey tests that gave neither "readable plaintext" nor "hints at the presence of a second encipherment layer," and the question "Could there possibly be a missing letter in the remaining section?" — with the classified methods tried redacted ([NSA summary](https://web.archive.org/web/20220514060258id_/https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/cia-kryptos-sculpture/KRYPTOS_Summary.pdf)). Gillogly reported in 1999 "on the order of 20 billion trial decryptions spread over two dozen different systems," and by 2005 "at least 100 billion possible solutions" ([WashPost 1999](https://web.archive.org/web/20160616083312/http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/national/daily/july99/kryptos19.htm); [WSJ via Post-Gazette 2005](https://web.archive.org/web/20070217114750id_/http://www.post-gazette.com/pg/05147/511693.stm)).

The cribs themselves do most of the exclusion work, and the research team re-derived their consequences as a reproducible check. For any periodic additive cipher over a single fixed alphabet (standard or KRYPTOS; Vigenère, Beaufort or variant), the 24 cribs are consistent only with periods 27, 28, 29 and 53–97; every period from 1 to 26 and 30 to 52 is contradicted, because the contradictions come from same-plaintext/different-ciphertext and same-ciphertext/different-plaintext pairs at congruent positions and are alphabet-independent. This is our own computation, consistent with Bean's published statement that Gromark periods 21 and 42 are excluded ([Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)). The implied Vigenère key fragments under the sculpture's own tableau are non-English under every convention (Schridde found MUYKLGKOR for BERLINCLOCK under the standard tabula recta and SYMLWRHZN under the statue tableau), so "plain Vigenère with an English keyword" is effectively dead ([Numberworld 2017](https://www.numberworld.blog/2017/03/kryptos-cipher-part-2.html)). The adjacent collisions (Q,Q→N,O; S,S→S,T; T,T→I,N) mean any solution's key state must change between every adjacent position, excluding slowly varying clock-driven schedules. Pure transposition is excluded twice over: the positional mismatch, and the letter count (the cribs contain three E's; the ciphertext has two) ([Numberworld 2020 comments](https://www.numberworld.blog/2020/07/kryptos-cipher-part-4.html)).

The absence is itself a signal: the bottleneck is the hypothesis space (which system, and what the mask does to the plaintext), not search power.

## Sanborn builds instruments and hides their output

Herbert James Sanborn Jr., born November 14, 1945 in Washington, is the only child of two Library of Congress employees — a printmaker father who directed exhibitions for 30 years and a concert-pianist mother who worked as a photo researcher — and he repeatedly cites the "institutional green hallways" of the federal government as the source of his raw material: "data, science, technology, war, conspiracy, espionage" ([FT Weekend 2025](https://freight.cargo.site/m/S2500073053613302968094958969384/kryptos.pdf); [Elonka Sanborn page](https://www.elonka.com/kryptos/sanborn.html)). He trained in archaeology, paleontology and anthropology before sculpture (Randolph-Macon 1968, an Oxford summer dig where he found a forgotten medieval crypt passage, Pratt MFA 1971), abandoned the human form after classmates savaged a representational head, and still calls his original training archaeology; the Carter passage in K3 is, in his words, "a moment of discovery" whose "magic" he wanted "to demonstrate for everyone, once it was cracked" ([Wikipedia Jim Sanborn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Sanborn); [CNN 2005 transcript](https://elonka.com/x/2005CNN.html)). His 1980s work made the Earth's invisible forces visible — lodestones hauled from a Utah lightning field, arrays of compass needles, the Coriolis force — and he won the **$250,000 CIA commission in November 1988** "on the back of his invisible-forces work and the fact that he was a DC kid," spending the entire fee on the sculpture ([jimsanborn.net](https://jimsanborn.net/content.html); [FT Weekend 2025](https://freight.cargo.site/m/S2500073053613302968094958969384/kryptos.pdf); [CIA legacy page](https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/kryptos-sculpture/)).

He sorts his own career into decade-long buckets, and every bucket turns on something present but not perceivable without an instrument. The 1990s, "starting with Kryptos, concerned the invisible forces of humans": Covert Obsolescence used pulped CIA documents; the Cyrillic Projector (installed 1997) and Antipodes carry Vigenère-type ciphertext of real KGB correspondence about source-handling and Andrei Sakharov's 1982 Pugwash report, broken by Frank Corr and Mike Bales in 2003 with Dunin's group supplying the translation ([FT Weekend 2025](https://freight.cargo.site/m/S2500073053613302968094958969384/kryptos.pdf); [Cyrillic Projector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrillic_Projector)). The mid-1990s Topographic Projections cast perfect geometric forms onto buttes from a quarter-mile away and survive only as photographs. The 2000s turned to atomic science: Critical Assembly rebuilt the Los Alamos assembly room from objects that "walked up out of their basements," and Terrestrial Physics was a working million-volt Van de Graaff accelerator that repeated the Carnegie Institution's January 1939 fission experiment — "I fissioned uranium in my studio using that machine" — which Blake Gopnik called "possibly the most substantial work of art to come out of Washington since the 1950s" ([AHF interview](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/voices/oral-histories/jim-sanborns-interview/); [Terrestrial Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_Physics); [Art Market Monitor](https://www.artmarketmonitor.com/2009/08/27/can-a-particle-accelerator-be-art/)). The 2010s "Without Provenance" commissioned Cambodian master forgers to carve Khmer antiquities, which he exhibited as a simulated auction with fabricated provenance; a critic called him an "ontological trouble-maker" with "a prankster's delight" and an interest in secrets ([Sculpture magazine 2019](https://sculpturemagazine.art/jim-sanborn/)). The Rhizome 2012 Q&A titled his program "Hidden Information": before Kryptos, unseen natural forces; since, hidden content in text and language ([Rhizome 2012](https://rhizome.org/editorial/2012/jan/26/jim-sanborn/)).

The pattern that bears on K4 is that Sanborn builds a working instrument from a scientist's design and then personalises it so the output is his alone — a compass array for a field, an accelerator for fission, a cipher for a text. He has said all four Kryptos passages "are fiction writings of mine," that "I do make stuff up," that he manipulates information "in a sort of disinformation fashion," and that misspellings are "an ancient tool for making codes more difficult to crack" ([CNN 2005 transcript](https://elonka.com/x/2005CNN.html); [FT Weekend 2025](https://freight.cargo.site/m/S2500073053613302968094958969384/kryptos.pdf)). The CIA's page still repeats a 1989 claim that the text was "written in collaboration with a prominent fiction writer"; Sanborn's answer is that it was an early idea he dropped: "why let someone else in on the secret?" ([CIA legacy page](https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/kryptos-sculpture/); [Elonka FAQ](https://elonka.com/kryptos/faq.html)). The K4 text itself was composed last, on the slow truck drive back from Holbrook, Arizona with the petrified tree in the back: "The last thing I wrote was on that drive" ([FT Weekend 2025](https://freight.cargo.site/m/S2500073053613302968094958969384/kryptos.pdf)). The auction inventory — separate items for "handwritten K4 plaintext," "the original K4 coding system," and a 1988 "alternate K4 plaintext and coding chart now known as K5" — implies a write-then-encrypt workflow with the texts fixed in 1988 and K4 and K5 conceived as siblings from the outset ([RR Auction lot](https://www.rrauction.com/auctions/lot-detail/350761607302001-the-complete-secrets-of-kryptos-jim-sanborns-private-archive/)). His two named K4 events, Egypt 1986 and the Wall in 1989, bracket the commission and sit inside one semantic field — overwritten strata, excavation, orientation, time — that also contains PALIMPSEST, ABSCISSA, LAYER TWO, the compass rose, and NORTHEAST ([Scientific American Nov 2025](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cia-kryptos-puzzle-creator-releases-final-clues/)).

His relationship to the secret is the other constant. "Secrecy is power, that's the unfortunate reality… And I have been resented for having this secret"; "Retaining an aura of mystery is, I think, good for any art"; "Once an artwork loses its mystery, it's lost a lot"; "This is a piece that requires two artists: encoder and decoder" ([FT Weekend 2025](https://freight.cargo.site/m/S2500073053613302968094958969384/kryptos.pdf); [CNN 2005 transcript](https://elonka.com/x/2005CNN.html); [Wired 2009 via metzdowd](https://www.metzdowd.com/pipermail/cryptography/2009-April/015266.html)). He claims not to remember the plaintext ("you read the piece of paper, you burn it, and you forget it") while knowing enough to vet submissions, suspected Dunin of being "a spy in her own right" waiting for a slip, and by 2025 described the stewardship as "servitude," a decade of cancer treatment, no feeling "from my navel down," death threats, and "Kryptos crazies that want to kill me" ([Wired 2005](https://www.wired.com/2005/01/questions-for-kryptos-creator/); [FT Weekend 2025](https://freight.cargo.site/m/S2500073053613302968094958969384/kryptos.pdf)). Dunin's explanation of why K4 is hard is the artist's, not the cryptographer's: "Because it was made by Jim, and he is not a cryptographer. He is an artist, and I think he did something goofy" ([FT Weekend 2025](https://freight.cargo.site/m/S2500073053613302968094958969384/kryptos.pdf)).

## Scheidt assembles keys from parts and judges solutions by method

Edward Michael Scheidt, born July 20, 1939 in San Bernardino County and schooled at Cor Jesu in New Orleans, went from Army Signals Intelligence in 1957 to the CIA's Office of Communications in 1963, spent **12 of 26 years overseas** under State Department cover (Vientiane, Damascus, Tel Aviv, Manila, Athens), "mostly used one-time pad paper encryption systems," rose to chairman of the agency's Cryptographic Center, and retired in December 1989, weeks before the Kryptos work ([Wikipedia Edward Scheidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Scheidt); [TecSec](https://tecsec.com/meet-the-team/)). Webster called him "The Wizard of Codes"; Bill Gertz of the Washington Times coined "Deep Throat of Codes" in 1991; the Washington Post described "a quiet, professorial man who favors ties with hieroglyphic patterns" ([WashPost 1999](https://web.archive.org/web/20160616083312/http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/national/daily/july99/kryptos19.htm); [Wikipedia Edward Scheidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Scheidt)). His formative cryptography is operational field communications — manual systems for officers in embassies — not cipher-machine design or academic cryptanalysis, which is consistent with everything he says about K4: hand-executable, "can be remembered," "what would be provided agents or pilots in case of capture."

In 1990 he co-founded TecSec in Vienna, Virginia, built the first portable-satellite STU-III in his basement, and spent three decades on "Constructive Key Management," in which symmetric keys are never transmitted but constructed on the client by combining multiple secret "splits" through a combiner, with access rules riding inside the encrypted object; the FIPS 140-2 policy describes "construction/reconstruction of 512 bit symmetric keys while only transferring benign information between the participants," and the architecture became ANSI X9.69 and X9.73 ([GCN 1999](https://www.route-fifty.com/digital-government/1999/09/key-encrypts-authenticates-on-clientnot-server/311952/); [FIPS policy](https://sec-certs.org/fips/c2077253a9b10166/target.txt)). He holds roughly **36 cryptographic patents from 1993 to 2015** — the key-split combiner family US6608901, US7079653, US7095851/2 and US8712046 is the technical statement of CKM — and as of 2023 was ANSI X9F vice chair and convenor of the ISO working group on digital-currency security ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/?inventor=Edward+M.+Scheidt&assignee=Tecsec&num=100); [Wikipedia Edward Scheidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Scheidt)). The design signature is "many small secrets combined deterministically into one working key, with the rules of access riding inside the ciphertext," and his professional vocabulary of "masking" is close to making key material look benign. Translated to a hand cipher, this is a man who thinks in terms of multiple independent key components that must all be known, a combiner step, and a label that tells you which components apply — which matches "more than one stage," "solve the technique first and then go for the puzzle," and the second-hand 2003 notes that "some of the code was designed to work with multiple keys" and "the key is the method rather than a keyword" ([scirealm hints](https://scirealm.org/KryptosHints.html)). His career-long preference is for systems the right party can reconstruct, not one-way puzzles: "don't create something that you cannot break — but at the same time, make it something that will last a while" ([Wired 2009 via metzdowd](https://www.metzdowd.com/pipermail/cryptography/2009-April/015266.html)).

His account of the Kryptos design is consistent and self-limiting. The CIA paired him with an artist who "had never used encryption or text in his work before"; they met "at secret locations around the region," wrote very little down, left electronic devices behind, and "used voice as the medium" because "the media had listening devices that could have picked up our conversation"; he spent about four months teaching pencil-and-paper systems "from the late 1800s through WWII" and designing processes "that had a historic basis, that didn't compromise any current methods," intending "something that could eventually be deciphered or extracted, rather than something that will never be done, ever" ([Northern Virginia Magazine 2022](https://northernvirginiamag.com/culture/2022/10/14/cia-kryptos-sculpture-cipher/); [WashPost 1999](https://web.archive.org/web/20160616083312/http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/national/daily/july99/kryptos19.htm)). "There are four different processes. Two of them are similar and the other two are different things," which maps onto Vigenère, Vigenère, transposition, and K4-as-something-else; he showed Sanborn "the how" and Sanborn chose "the what"; K1–K3 "were designed not to last a long time," and he expected the whole puzzle solved in "roughly five years," "less than seven," or "five, seven or maybe ten," rating K4 about nine out of ten ([kryptools](https://www.kryptools.com.about.ag/hints.htm); [Baltimore Sun 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/20140209165252/http://articles.baltimoresun.com/2000-03-17/news/0003180448_1_decipher-petrified-wood-cia-headquarters); [The Conversation 2021](https://theconversation.com/declassified-cold-war-code-breaking-manual-has-lessons-for-solving-impossible-puzzles-161595)). He verified K1–K3 during development but "intentionally did not" check K4 — "if Jim was going to do something then OK Jim does something and then I can honestly say well I only know what I did" — and has "never" been tempted to attack it: "I'm a code creator, not a code breaker" ([Workshop transcript 2015](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/kryptos-workshop-transcript/); [Northern Virginia Magazine 2022](https://northernvirginiamag.com/culture/2022/10/14/cia-kryptos-sculpture-cipher/)). He has flagged repeatedly that Sanborn's execution may diverge from his design: "since he's the one who had the chisel in his hands, there could be some changes" ([scirealm hints](https://scirealm.org/KryptosHints.html)).

His personality is dry, withholding, and loyal. "The last 97 characters? Don't hold your breath… I saved the best for last… No clues, sorry" (1999); "the fun part is to see how the folks approach the problem"; "I've had others say you definitely have a convoluted mind"; "it's a serious business playing spy"; he deflects specifics as "a Jim question," and when a dinner guest asked whether K4 was a one-off, he "looked to Jim and said 'well Jim, how are we going to answer that one?' To which Jim simply replied 'we don't answer it'" ([WashPost 1999](https://web.archive.org/web/20160616083312/http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/national/daily/july99/kryptos19.htm); [Workshop transcript 2015](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/kryptos-workshop-transcript/); [rumkin K5](https://rumkin.com/reference/kryptos/k5/)). He does not do crosswords, prefers anonymity ("I really don't advertise"), and in 2014 said with a chuckle that he would not have expected people "still banging their heads against Kryptos so many years later… But a technique that I used obviously worked" ([Northern Virginia Magazine 2022](https://northernvirginiamag.com/culture/2022/10/14/cia-kryptos-sculpture-cipher/); [NYT 2014](https://web.archive.org/web/20240224133656id_/https://www.nytimes.com/2014/11/21/us/another-kryptos-clue-is-offered-in-a-24-year-old-mystery-at-the-cia.html)). His verdict to Stein — "the solution is correct, but you didn't do it the way I intended" — shows he has always cared about method over plaintext, which is exactly the community's post-2025 position. He appears to be alive at 87 (a patent application published in 2025 lists him as inventor; in-person interview in 2022; ANSI activity in 2023), but no statement from him about the auction, the Smithsonian find, Paradigm or K5 has surfaced ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/?inventor=Edward+M.+Scheidt&assignee=Tecsec&num=100); [Wikipedia Edward Scheidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Scheidt)).

## The object: through-cut letters, a mirrored tableau, Morse pages and an offset compass

Kryptos is a two-part installation. At the entrance to the New Headquarters Building, tilted red-granite "pages" flank the walkway from the parking deck with a copper sheet sandwiched between them, perforated with International Morse, beside a granite slab engraved with a compass rose whose needle points at a lodestone; in the northwest corner of the courtyard, an 11-foot column of petrified wood supports an S-shaped screen of four half-inch copper plates, 11–12 feet by 20 feet, curling around a bubbling pool, with a calm reflecting pool between two blank granite outcrops nearby ([CIA legacy page](https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/kryptos-sculpture/); [Elonka aerial page](https://elonka.com/kryptos/KryptosAerial.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos)). Sanborn's December 1989 letter to agency employees explains the symbolism: "the tilted strata tell a story like pages of a document"; the code "begins as International Morse and increases in complexity as you move through the piece at the entrance and into the courtyard"; the lodestone "refers to ancient navigational compasses"; the petrified tree recalls the trees that were "the source of materials on which written language has been recorded," the copper "represents this 'paper,'" and the plaza pool is "turbulent and provocative" while the courtyard pool is "calm, reflective, contemplative" ([Cryptogram 1991](https://www.thekryptosproject.com/kryptos/cia/thecryptogram/pdfs/Binder2.pdf)). The CIA reads the bubbling pool as "information being disseminated with the destination being unknown" ([CIA legacy page](https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/kryptos-sculpture/)).

Viewed from the courtyard with the petrified tree on the left, the **left half carries the ciphertext (two plates of 14 lines, 869 characters: 865 letters and four question marks)** and the **right half the keyed Vigenère tableau (867 letters)**: a plain A–Z header, 26 rows beginning with successive left-shifts of KRYPTOSABCDEFGHIJLMNQUVWXZ, and a footer, with row N carrying the extra L ([Elonka transcript](https://elonka.com/kryptos/transcript.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos)). Every letter was jigsaw-cut through the copper over two and a half years, "five days a week, 52 weeks a year," which is why Sanborn says "You could not make any mistake with 1,800 letters. It could not be repaired" — a remark about fabrication, not about the charts ([SPYSCAPE](https://spyscape.com/article/kryptos-spy-art-how-the-cia-sculptor-teased-the-worlds-top-brains); [NYT 2025 via Schneier](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2025/08/jim-sanborn-is-auctioning-off-the-solution-to-part-four-of-the-kryptos-sculpture.html)). Because the letters are through-cut, every letter is legible from both sides, one of them mirrored, and the CIA states that the tableau "is intentionally flipped so it can only be read from the back of the sculpture": the ciphertext reads correctly from the courtyard and the key reads correctly from behind, so using the sculpture's own key literally requires walking around the S ([CIA legacy page](https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/kryptos-sculpture/)). No analyst publication documents a mirror-image letter reading as a cipher step; every "reversal" reference in the literature concerns the physical orientation of the tableau. Nothing on the screen is embossed; the only "raised" letters, YAR, are raised in position, not relief, and Dunin's 2002 rubbings put them "a couple centimeters out of alignment" ([Elonka main page](https://elonka.com/kryptos/)). Dunin's visit notes record that afternoon sun passes through the letters, that green-tinted windows reflect letter-shaped light onto a white rock beside the petrified "gnomon," and that the S-curve throws narrow bars of light she compared to Morse or I Ching patterns; no sun-angle or shadow analysis has been published, and "shadows and sundials" and "holding a candle up to the sculpture" remain listed only as proposed theories ([Elonka main page](https://elonka.com/kryptos/); [Elonka FAQ](https://elonka.com/kryptos/faq.html)).

The Morse on the entrance slabs, read from the rock outward, yields seven fragments padded with lone dits read as E's: E E VIRTUALLY E / E E E E E E INVISIBLE; DIGETAL E E E / INTERPRETATI (cut off at the stone, rendered INTERPRETATU, INTERPRETATIT or INTERPRETATION by different transcribers); E E SHADOW E E / FORCES E E E E E; LUCID E E E / MEMORY E; T IS YOUR / POSITION E (the T likely the tail of a longer word, with "WHAT IS YOUR POSITION" a community reconstruction); SOS; and RQ, which reads YR upside down ([rumkin K0](https://rumkin.com/reference/kryptos/k0/); [Elonka FAQ](https://elonka.com/kryptos/faq.html); [Kryptos Project K0](https://thekryptosproject.com/kryptos/k0-k5/k0.php)). DIGETAL is the second documented misspelling on the site after the three in K1–K3; the NSA's 1991 study-group notes already recorded the same readings ([NSA summary](https://web.archive.org/web/20220514060258id_/https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/cia-kryptos-sculpture/KRYPTOS_Summary.pdf)). The compass rose is deliberately offset: Sanborn told NOVA the dial "is not oriented north and south. It's offset… there's a mystery there," one visitor estimated its "north" at roughly 220 degrees, the Kryptos Project estimates the needle parallel to the front sidewalk, and the Wall Street Journal reported the needle "is pulled off due north by a lodestone that Mr. Sanborn placed nearby" ([Kryptos Project strata](https://thekryptosproject.com/kryptos/pok/strata.php); [Elonka aerial page](https://elonka.com/kryptos/KryptosAerial.html); [WSJ via Post-Gazette 2005](https://web.archive.org/web/20070217114750id_/http://www.post-gazette.com/pg/05147/511693.stm)). No surveyed bearing exists, so the community claim that EASTNORTHEAST "points in the same direction as a compass etched into a stone" cannot be reconciled with the 220-degree estimate and should not be fed to a loop as fact ([rumkin K4](https://rumkin.com/reference/kryptos/k4/)). The K2 coordinates fall roughly 100 to 174 feet southeast of the screen depending on datum, in or near an adjacent enclosed courtyard with a manhole cover; Sanborn has reportedly said the "buried item" there was a half-buried bronze USGS marker he used to pace off the location, since removed, and that any further buried clue would have had to be "ephemeral" to pass the CIA's nightly scans ([Elonka main page](https://elonka.com/kryptos/); [rumkin K5](https://rumkin.com/reference/kryptos/k5/); [Scientific American Nov 2025](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cia-kryptos-puzzle-creator-releases-final-clues/)). The K4 section of the screen is "largely — and purposefully — obscured by a rock," and the stones were placed "so that a photo could not capture all of the elements" ([FT Weekend 2025](https://freight.cargo.site/m/S2500073053613302968094958969384/kryptos.pdf); [WashPost 1999](https://web.archive.org/web/20160616083312/http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/national/daily/july99/kryptos19.htm)). Antipodes at the Hirshhorn repeats the Kryptos text with the extra L and the UUND/RTBJ spelling intact and is the only independent "transcription" in copper ([Antipodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipodes_(sculpture)); [Bean 2018](https://kryptos.richardbean.id.au/kryptos-2018.htm)).

## Conclusion

The assumption "K4 plaintext is readable English you either have or don't" is safe as a description of the artefact and unsafe as a definition of success, and the two halves should be engineered separately. As an artefact, K4 is a fixed 97-letter English phrase with 24 letters pinned, now guarded by an exact-match oracle at $1 a query, and the evidence that it is English is as strong as it can be short of publication: the people who hold it say it "makes semantic sense," and the cribs are ordinary words at consistent offsets. But the oracle verifies a string Sanborn typed, not a decipherment, so a loop needs its own canonicalisation tests and must assume the true string may contain one deliberate misspelling in the K1–K3 pattern, Scheidt's "mask" (plausibly nulls, abbreviation, phonetic spelling, or a changed "language base"), and no spaces or punctuation beyond the X-as-stop convention. A scorer that rewards only clean dictionary English can rank the right answer below wrong ones, exactly as three expert teams ranked ID BY ROWS above the truth for years; the better design is to make the 24 cribs, the letter-count constraints, and the adjacent-position key-change requirement hard filters, score the remaining 73 letters with a model tolerant of orthographic noise, and spend oracle queries only on fully specified candidates that survive all filters.

What counts as success has moved twice. From 1992 to 2010 it was fluent text plus a replicable method, with the author's silence as assent; from 2010 to 2025 it was Sanborn's positional spot-check and the community's "plaintext with cribs in place, a method, and enough explanation for independent reproduction"; since October 2025 it is words plus method plus the understanding that the words are a riddle pointing to K5, with the artist, the auction house, the community's gatekeeper and Scientific American all on record that a plaintext without a method is "discovered, not solved." That reframing creates alternative forms of success the evidence supports and that an automated loop is well placed to pursue even without a verifier pass: a documented, bounded exhaustion of the open classical families (keyed periodic systems at periods 27–29 and 53 and above, Gromark and LFSR variants beyond base 10, progressive and autokey forms), which would be a publishable negative result in the tradition of Bean's 2021 paper; a construction-simulation study in the Oranchak style that identifies which generators causally reproduce K4's width-7 and width-21 doublet properties, narrowing the method space; a crib-consistent method that regenerates the 24 known letters and produces a candidate for the rest, which the oracle can then confirm or deny; and, once Paradigm releases K5, a method that explains both 97-letter ciphertexts with BERLINCLOCK at the same positions, which is the strongest form of verification available because it is independent of anyone's typed string. The deepest implication of the record is that Kryptos was never designed as a self-contained cryptogram but as a staged object whose author values the condition of being-a-secret over the content of the secret; the loop that respects that will measure itself by what it can prove about the method and the mask, and treat the words as the last confirmation rather than the goal.
