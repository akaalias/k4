# 07. People

**Jim Sanborn** (Herbert James Sanborn Jr., born November 14, 1945, Washington). The
author. Trained in archaeology, paleontology and anthropology before sculpture; Pratt MFA
1971. Wrote all four plaintexts himself ("fiction writings of mine") in 1988, K4 last, on
the drive back from Arizona with the petrified tree. Took Scheidt's systems and "modified
them to my own ends"; "I fucked with it." Calls himself an "anathemath." Verified claims
by positional letter check, then a $50 fee, then sold the archive. Deals in
"disinformation" by his own account and says so. See `10-sanborn-the-artist.md`.

**Ed Scheidt** (born 1939). The system designer. Field communications officer, one-time
pads, chairman of the CIA's Cryptographic Center, retired December 1989, TecSec founder,
key-splitting patents. Taught Sanborn hand systems from the late 1800s to WWII over four
months in 1989, by voice, at secret locations. Never checked K4. "I only know what I did."
See `03-scheidt-said.md`.

**William Webster.** CIA director 1987-91. Received a sealed envelope (or two) at the
dedication: keywords and plaintext per Bauer and AP; "I definitely didn't give him the
last section" per Sanborn. "I have zero memory of this. It was philosophical and obscure."
Died in 2025 at 101. WW in K2.

**The NSA task force, 1992.** Z Group cryptanalysts after a challenge from CIA deputy
director William Studeman relayed by NSA director Mike McConnell. Ken Miller headed and
wrote up; Dennis McDaniels solved K3 on his couch in six hours starting from the Q; two
unnamed analysts solved K2 and K1. Three parts in two days, one day on K4, then stopped.
Documents released by FOIA in 2013-14 to Elonka Dunin and John Greenewald.

**David Stein.** CIA physicist and analyst. Solved K1-K3 by pencil over 400 hours,
breakthrough February 21, 1998. Split the panel at its question marks, found key lengths
by index of coincidence, used log-frequency sums and digraph tables. "Kryptos was meant to
be solved with pencil and paper." Noted the I.C. peak at m = 13. Scheidt told him his
solution was correct "but you didn't do it the way I intended."

**Jim Gillogly.** Computer scientist, ACA past president. Solved K1-K3 by computer in
nine days in June 1999 from Doug Gwyn's transcription; first to publish checkably.
Suggested running key, substitution plus transposition, autokey, period 25, Gromark;
flagged "Yes, wonderful things" as a possible crib. Ran tens of billions of trials. Typed
the plaintext into Paradigm's verifier in 2026 per one account (Paradigm says Sanborn did).

**Doug Gwyn.** Posted the first clean transcription to sci.crypt in 1992; it was the
standard for a decade but omitted the extra L. Examined delta-text I.C.; thinks K4 will
fall to "somebody versed in classical C/A who happens to make a lucky guess."

**Elonka Dunin.** Game developer, co-founder and moderator of the Kryptos group since
2003, keeper of the timeline, FAQ, transcript, rubbings (2002) and the hints mirrors.
Visited Sanborn and Scheidt in 2003; hosts the Kryptos dinners; filed the NSA FOIA.
Co-author of Codebreaking (2020). Her standard: plausible plaintext with the cribs in
place, a method, and enough explanation for a third party to verify. "If they don't have
the method, it's not solved." "He is an artist, and I think he did something goofy."
Sanborn suspected her of waiting for him to slip.

**Klaus Schmeh.** German cryptologist, Cipherbrain blog, co-author of Codebreaking.
Hosted the 2015 workshop; records each clue and the failed attempts in his comments.

**Richard Bean.** University of Queensland. The most rigorous published analyst:
"Kryptos Thoughts" (2018), the HistoCrypt 2021 cryptodiagnosis, the Conversation piece,
the Gromark search with code on GitHub (RichardBean/k4testing). Believes one-to-one, no
transposition, Gromark-like; suspects a mistake in K4 or an unsolvable homebrew.

**Craig Bauer.** Cryptologia editor, York College of Pennsylvania. Hill-cipher
conjecture from the HILL column (2016, 2017). "Firmly rooted in the world of paper and
pencil encryption." Expects "yet another mystery" beyond K4.

**Christian Schridde.** Numberworld blog. Technical write-ups on K1-K3 and K4 (2017,
2020), the 3x3 Hill contradiction, Wichmann-Hill, Scott's affine construction, the 5-bit
ASCII analysis, the "Kryptos as a coordinate system" idea (ABSCISSA, ORDINATE).

**Ferdinando Stehle, Barry Mapen, Neil D'Costa, Sean O'Neil, David Allen Wilson, Chris
Hanson, Monet Friedrich, Roger Anderson, Ed Hannon, Greg Materna, Keith Edkins, Gary
Warzin, John Wilson, Bill Briere, Jew-Lee Lann Briere.** Community members who found,
respectively: the 192 formula for K3; the earlier K3 formula; the K3 column key equal to
KRYPTOS; the "fold the halves" layer-two theory; PLAYERTWO from rotating ABSCISSA (October
2005); the extra L (2003); the width-21 repeats and the 2010-11 dinner reports; the
KRYPTOS-letter closeness; the 2x2 Hill exhaustion (2004); the original Yahoo group and the
suspicion that K4 "is actually really easy... a Eureka moment"; the hints compilation and
the "Carter's candle as instructions" theory; the Kryptos Project site.

**Jarett Kobek and Richard Byrne.** Writers who recovered the K4 words from Sanborn's
Smithsonian papers in September 2025 by library science, refused NDAs and a share of the
auction, were threatened by the auction house's lawyers, and have not published. Kobek:
"this is not necessarily something that's ever going to be solved by cryptanalysis or
STEM or math."

**Paradigm** (Dan Robinson, Matt Huang). Bought the archive for $962,500 in November
2025, disclosed itself in June 2026, holds only an HMAC of the plaintext, runs the $1
verifier and a ten-puzzle CTF, and holds the unreleased K5 ciphertext.

**Dave Oranchak, Sam Blake, Jarl Van Eycke.** Solved Zodiac 340 in 2020 by simulating
cipher construction until a generator reproduced the ciphertext's statistical fingerprint.
The method, not the people, matters here.
