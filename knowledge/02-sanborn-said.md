# 02. What Sanborn has said that bears on K4

Chronological. Verbatim unless marked. Sources in `sources/`. Sanborn has said he deals
in "disinformation" (2005), so weigh each line, but note that the method statements are
remarkably stable across 35 years.

## 1989, letter to agency employees (December 15)

"The tilted strata tell a story like pages of a document." "This code, which includes
certain ancient ciphers, begins as International Morse and increases in complexity as you
move through the piece." "The right side is a text that can be partly deciphered by using
the table and partly by using a potentially challenging encoding system." "My choice of
materials, like code, conveys meaning. At the entrance a lodestone... refers to ancient
navigational compasses. The petrified tree recalls the trees... that were the source of
materials on which written language has been recorded. The copper, perforated by text,
represents this 'paper'."

## 1990, Washington Post (January, via the NSA's slides)

"Anyone who knows a coding system called the Vigenere Tableau... will be able to decipher
one-half of the phrase. The other half will be encoded in a modern system created for the
project by an expert cryptographer."

## 1990-91, dedication and AP (via the Cryptogram, 1991)

"Its placement in geologic context reinforces the text's hiddenness as if it were a
fossil, frozen in time." To AP: "They will be able to read what I wrote, but what I wrote
is a mystery itself... People will always say, 'What did he mean by that?' What I wrote
out were clues to a larger mystery." AP also reported two sealed envelopes to Webster,
"one contained the message translation while the other contained the keywords."

## 1991, ABC World News Tonight (April 2) and B-roll

"Once the plate is deciphered I'm not convinced the true meaning will be clear even then.
There's another deeper mystery." B-roll: "I've made a statement which is straightforward,
but that leads to something else... As you peel off a layer of an onion... you get closer
to the heart of what it is... It's in English, it's in plain English, it's in text, and
you can read it, but that isn't necessarily the whole story." On the message: "It is either
a quotation or it's something I've written, or it's something somebody else has written.
But let's just say, that it has a lot to do with secrecy. It has a lot to do with finding
something, or opening something, or discovering something that's secret." On design:
"Part of it was designed to be deciphered within a couple of weeks. Part of it was designed
to be deciphered within a couple of months. And part of it within years, or never." "And
so I chose that word [Kryptos] as my keyword for the code also... the keyword for some of
that text is Kryptos."

## 1992-93, exhibition catalogues

Covert Obsolescence: "The words and paragraphs that I selected are very straightforward;
I've hidden them from view because I am deliberately secretive about the content of my
work." Secrets Passed (curator Sue Scott): "As an archeology student in England, Sanborn
found ancient ruins by flying over the landscape either early or late in the day. It was
only when the sun was raking across the landscape that stone circles cast shadows and the
buried structures and fortifications could be seen... This concept of information and/or
objects that can disappear or appear depending on the manipulation of light sources is a
recurring theme in much of Sanborn's work." The Code Room's key "is the Russian word TEHb
meaning shadow."

## 1999, NPR and Washington Post and New York Times

NPR: "There are encoding systems which use pattern, light and shadow, and it's those kinds
of systems that I was most interested in using, because I'm an artist... and I chose not
to use, in at least what's been deciphered already, perhaps what has not been
deciphered... I like to use spatial systems of encoding and decoding." Asked if the K1
sentence is itself a clue: "It's possible, anything's possible." Post, via Schwartz:
Sanborn did the actual encryption himself "during a four-day jag trucking the huge piece
of petrified tree from Arizona"; "Sanborn had placed the stones around the sculpture
strategically, so that a photo could not capture all of the elements... most
[transcriptions] did not fully show the slight misalignment of some letters along the
matrix, which might figure in unlocking the secrets." Times, via Markoff: "a riddle within
a riddle, one that will be solvable only after the four encrypted passages are known";
"he believes that the ultimate secret hidden in the text of Kryptos will never be
deciphered."

## 2003, Atomic Time catalogue

"I wrote a fairly extensive text, then encoded it into a matrix system, which seemed to
me as an artist, to be fairly simple."

## 2003, Dunin's road trip (secondhand, Dunin's notes via Wilson)

He "kept using that word 'matrix' quite a bit, such as to say 'matrix system'," said it
was odd that "no one has recovered the original matrix," and "was surprised that no one
had tried recovering the original matrix and running it through all the possible
'shifts'." Shown the out-of-alignment letters: "They're important." Misspellings were
deliberate "but it was less important what they were, but 'it's more the orientation of
those letters that's useful there'... the 'positioning' that was important." Scheidt
taught him systems "going back to Caesar"; he used "several"; the encryption was done by
himself on the Arizona trip; the two kept it "compartmentalized"; no one beta-tested the
code; he was "certain that he got it right." Scheidt had told him misspellings make things
harder. K2's coordinates "could actually be one of two different locations, either
Xenon's spot, or one near the front entrance area." The front-entrance pieces "were
supposed to parallel something in the courtyard." The K1 poem was "an original sentence,
written by himself, with 'carefully-chosen wording'." K2 was written to sound like "an
interrupted radio transmission, similar to the Morse code messages." He and Scheidt knew
INVISIBLE is a Morse palindrome. He declined to say whether the final "?" belongs to K3
or K4, and whether DESPARATLY was a typo. Asked flat out in mid-2003 whether K4 was
solvable: "Yes. It ain't easy, but it's solvable!"

## 2005, Wired (January 20, Zetter)

"For the student of cryptography it's always helpful to gather as much information as
possible when zeroing in on and encoding a system." Other sculptures as clues: "Well, I'm
not going to answer that question." Entrance to courtyard: "this part is easy, the next
part is less easy, the next part is the most difficult." Must you be on the grounds? "No."
"That doesn't mean that what I've said in the piece doesn't do something physically there
at the agency." "In part of the code that's been deciphered, I refer to an act that took
place when I was at the agency and a location that's on the grounds of the agency. So...
you have to decipher the piece and then go to the agency and find that place." "An act
that I could have carried out." Techniques: "I would think five or six." "Mr. Scheidt
basically gave me an outline of historic and contemporary... encoding systems... He gave me
a whole variety of possible systems to use and ways to modify all of those systems. But as
a visual artist, I like to rely on systems that include visual (material) as well as
digital material that can be deciphered by machines. It's also well-known that I did use
some matrix codes Ed gave me, and I have also designed visual systems for encoding, which
are much harder for cryptographers to crack because they're individualistic." "No, he
doesn't know the solution... I'd be modifying systems and developing my own, which would
make it virtually impossible for him to decipher all of it. I intended the 80 percent...
to be deciphered in stages and relatively quickly. The final part is obviously... the apex
of the pyramid." "The answer will be far more ambiguous. Of the part that's been decoded
already there is certain ambiguity in the last few sentences." "If a person deciphers and
sends me the exact decipherment, if it can be deciphered exactly, considering most of my
things are rife with mistakes on purpose, I'd probably let them know that they got it if
they did. I will say that I have left instructions in the earlier text that refer to
later text." On the nightly scans: "it makes it difficult to do whatever you'd like to
do." "I definitely didn't give him [Webster] the last section." "I've got it hidden
someplace but I'm not going to read it. I have done everything I can to forget it."

## 2005, Wall Street Journal (May 27)

"The most obvious key to the sculpture, nobody has picked up on." He "withheld the full
solution to the puzzle from Mr. Scheidt, as well as from the CIA itself."

## 2005, NPR (June 9)

"This is a person, I am a person who has zero mathematical ability, took tutoring for
algorithms in high school." "I don't really believe I gave them the entire code."
He nods when asked whether all four passages will turn out to be linked and says whoever
cracks part four "will then be at the beginning of the puzzle."

## 2005, CNN transcript (mid-2005, Ensor, transcribed by Dunin)

On the Office of Historical Intelligence: he brought "the plaintext, but it was scrambled
in such a way that you could read sentences, but you wouldn't get the whole picture."
"When you decipher one part, it might hark back to the first part. And perhaps when the
fourth part is deciphered, what they call K4, it will come back to the first part, you
know, in some way, and there will be a relationship between all parts when the fourth part
is deciphered. I spent hours conceiving of this when driving back from Arizona." "I'm an
artist, okay? I'm not a mathematician, but I do have other skills, more visual skills,
that were brought to bear in designing the code." "Then he gave me the systems, I was able
to modify them to my own ends." "All of the passages are fiction writings of mine.
Whether they're related, the public won't know that until the fourth passage is
deciphered." Would knowing the first sections help? "Maybe." Clues on other parts of the
site? "Possibly." "You've been quoted as saying there's a clue somewhere in plain sight."
"Sure. Yeah, there definitely is... having the letters sitting there in plain sight is a
pretty big clue... As an artist I work with light and shadow, and all of these kinds of
things, smoke and mirrors." Misspellings: "That's an ancient tool for making codes more
difficult to crack. Saying I was sloppy is a good way of saying it... It's unpredictable.
Unpredictability is one of the strengths of difficult code." Count: "Whether it's 97,
whether it's 100, I seem to like to manipulate the information coming out, in a sort of
disinformation fashion... a lot of the clues that I might give work in a sort of
disinformational way." The "?": "It's a question mark." The method, pointing at the
Antipodes tableau: "That was one of the systems. I used several systems to encode this
piece. Then you use this chart to decipher the other code... But that might be deciphered
using this after I've already... this using a matrix or some other encoding system. So I
use a combination of systems. The matrix possibly, and then this, in combination with one
another... The people who decipher these have already determined that I used matrix
codes. Okay? Now matrix codes are a very specific type of encoding system, in which you
basically take a piece of paper, and you write a line of plain text out, right, in
successive rows. And then basically you write it going from top to bottom instead of left
to right... So basically, I used a matrix code, and then we can possibly use this code
[the tableau] to decipher with. So it was a variety of systems. And sometimes I used
double matrixes and triple matrixes in order to develop a complicated system, and then
went back to this table (pointing at Vigenere) to do the ultimate."

## 2006, Wired (April 20) and the Kryptos Group

"No, that last part is wrong." Clues to K4 "are contained in previously deciphered parts.
Therefore getting those first three sections correct is crucial" (via Zetter). "It bothers
me if they put 'id by rows' in instead of 'layer two'... They didn't have everything to
work with that I wanted them to have to work with." He had thought ID BY ROWS "was
cryptographer's jargon." He confirmed X LAYER TWO and "refused to reveal anything more."

## 2007, PBS NOVA (via Bean and the Kryptos Project)

Matrix codes (bar codes) were his favourite type of encryption, "being more art-like."
The compass dial "is not oriented north and south. It's offset... there's a mystery there."
He inserted copper perforated with Morse between granite slabs "like ore between stone or
pages of a book"; the outside outcroppings "line up with similar ones inside the Agency
grounds, as seen from the air."

## 2009, Wired (Levy) and the Smithsonian oral history

"A coding system that would unravel itself slowly over a period of time." "It's not my
intent to put out disinformation. I'm a benevolent cryptographer." "Nobody has it all. I
tricked them." "If somebody tried to torture me, I couldn't tell them... I haven't looked
at the plaintext of K4 in a long time." "In some ways, I'd rather die knowing it wasn't
cracked." Oral history (as quoted in 2020): "Ed basically gave me, he gave me a primer of
ancient encoding systems. And he also gave me some ideas for contemporary coding systems,
more sophisticated systems, systems that didn't necessarily depend on mathematics. That
was one of my prerequisites. So he told me about matrix codes and things like that. These
are the parts of Kryptos that have already been cracked. So I can discuss them. But he
told me about coding systems that I could then modify in a myriad of ways. So that even
he would not know what it says. Okay? So that was very seductive to me. And so I took
those things. We met two or three times. And that's what I based the whole thing on."
Also from the oral history, via the FT: "I didn't necessarily give them the whole code"
(to the CIA's historical office); Dunin "was a spy in her own right... to find out what the
code was if I made some slip."

## 2010, New York Times and NPR (November 20-22)

"I assumed the code would be cracked in a fairly short time." He "admitted to introducing
misspellings to add a degree of difficulty" (via Schwartz). "The other 91 characters and
their proper order are yet to be determined" (Schwartz's wording; note "proper order").
"My friend says, 'What do you have in letter-position 27?'... 'Game over.'" "So far, no
one has had more than two letters right." "It will not be plain as day, ever" (Wired,
November 2010). NPR: "I also divulged, or gave images, of my original decoding charts,
the ones that I, well, actually, for me they were encoding charts. And I think once the
Krypto-philes study it in a forensic manner, there might be revelations in there. So in a
way, I gave more than just Berlin." "It is a riddle within a riddle. And the final
passage will lead in several directions. The word directions is, I use intentionally."

## 2011, Kryptos dinner (secondhand, Hannon)

Reportedly "indicated this is a one-to-one mapping" from NYPVTT to BERLIN; at a 2013
dinner "rattled through the entire crib and said: N = B, Y = E, ..." At the 2013 dinner:
"can we solve K4 without solving K1-K3 first? Jim: Yes, but K5 cannot be solved without
K4." Something buried adjacent to Kryptos: "a bronze USGS marker. It was half buried. It
had little cross hairs on the top of it," since removed, "but it is important." He "still
believes there's something to be gained if we study his encryption charts... he brought up
his K1/K2 matrix and seemed mystified no one has gotten any thing more from it."

## 2012, Rhizome and a later statement

"For the past 30 years, my task as an artist has been to release this hidden information
at a rate commensurate with its importance, and at the time of my choosing so as to
prolong the experience of discovery."

## 2014, New York Times and Wired (November 20)

"There are several really interesting clocks in Berlin." "You'd better delve into that
particular clock," said of the Mengenlehreuhr. "There's a lot of fodder there." He was
designing the project when the Wall fell: "there's no doubt I was influenced by all that
going on simultaneously." $50 fee: "It really worked very well." Scheidt and Webster
"only thought they knew the solution. In fact, he had deceived them" (Wired's gloss).

## 2019, Dunin's lunch (March, secondhand) and Schmeh

"Jim was confused when we mentioned masking technique." He "backed off and said he would
only commit to the fact that K4 is exactly 97 characters long, and that BERLIN is
plaintext that starts at exactly the 64th character." To Schmeh: it is not necessary to
know the K1-K3 solutions to solve K4. Per Bogart: "BERLINCLOCK in plain matches directly
with NYPVTTMZFPK. It is a one-to-one match with plain B taken, has the encipherment done
to it, and out pops a cipher N, plain E is then enciphered to a cipher Y." Per Kobek
(2025): at a March 2019 dinner he said he "did not care" what method someone used.

## 2020, NPR (January 29) and the A.J. Jacobs interview

"It's a 97-character phrase. And that phrase is in itself a riddle. It's mysterious. It's
going to lead to something else. It's not going to be finished when it's decoded." "It is
very close, within days, to when I actually developed that 97-character string." "I am
lousy at math to the point where I consider myself to be anathamath. Now, perhaps being an
artist, I have an advantage in that I can employ methods that don't have anything to do
with mathematics." "Frequency of certain letters that correspond to the English language
don't really work well when you have a short passage. And so I don't know that I really at
that time thought about it, but it certainly has been the case." Jacobs: wanted to "make
it as difficult as possible," hoped K4 "would outlive him." Wrong guess example: "a very
elaborate scheme of KRYPTOS overlaying a satellite image of the CIA and somehow it made a
guitar."

## 2025, FT (August), open letter (August 14), Spy Museum (November 12), RR Auction

FT: "It is a solid system, and I fucked with it." "What do you mean?" "I can't say." "The
last thing I wrote was on that drive," meaning the K4 text, on the truck ride from
Holbrook. "Secrecy is power." "The thing about AI is it's lying, it's telling you what you
want to hear." Open letter: "the plain text, the secret of K4"; "I sincerely hope the new
owner will choose to keep K4 a secret. If they don't, then (CLUE) what's the point? Power
resides with a secret, not without it." The FT reporter calls that line "one final hint";
the parenthetical "(CLUE)" is in the original. "Even when K4 has been solved, its riddle
will persist as K5." Spy Museum, via Scientific American: "Who says it is even a math
solution?" "I was fortunate not to understand mathematics, probably in my ability to make
the code." He counselled "creativity." "K4 has not been solved or decrypted." RR Auction
blog: "What they found was scrambled text, and not by solving the cryptogram. They did not
solve K4 and they certainly did not find the key. This is a very important distinction."
"K4 has not been solved. K4 has been discovered and it points in the direction of K5."
AFP: "The scrambled plain text was found, but without the coding method or the key."
November letter via Popular Mechanics: "Two events had a significant role to play while I
was writing the plain text of Kryptos in 1988. The first was my second trip to Egypt in
late 1986, and the second was the fall of the Berlin Wall." "The Berlin Clock in K4 is the
World Clock in Berlin that was the gathering place for the crowds that brought down the
Berlin wall." "The codes of Kryptos from the morse code at the beginning through K5 are
about delivering a message." K5 "will have a more global reach," will be publicly
accessible, uses a similar system, and "even the words BERLINCLOCK will be in the same
position."
